\chapter{Magnetostatics}
\label{ch:em-magnetostatics}

Magnetostatics is the study of the magnetic fields of \emph{steady} currents. The chapter parallels electrostatics step by step: the force law, the source law (Biot--Savart in place of Coulomb), the field equations ($\div\vect{B}$ and $\curl\vect{B}$), a potential, and boundary conditions, ending with a side-by-side comparison.

\section{Currents and the Magnetic Force}

\begin{definition}[Magnetic field]
The magnetic field $\vect{B}$ is defined by the velocity-dependent part of the force on a test charge, the Lorentz force
\[
    \vect{F} = q\vect{E} + q\lvect{v}\times\vect{B},
\]
with $\vect{E}$ and $\vect{B}$ evaluated at the particle's position, in the frame in which $\lvect{v}$ is measured.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Line, surface and volume currents]
\[
    I = \lambda v,
    \qquad
    \vect{K} = \frac{d\vect{I}}{dl_\perp} = \sigma\lvect{v},
    \qquad
    \vect{J} = \frac{d\vect{I}}{da_\perp} = \rho\lvect{v}.
\]
$\vect{K}$ is the current per unit width perpendicular to the flow, and $\vect{J}$ the current per unit area.
\end{definition}

A wire element $d\lvect{s}$ carrying current $I = \lambda v$ holds charge $dq = \lambda\,ds$, and $dq\,\lvect{v} = I\,d\lvect{s}$. Summing the Lorentz force over the moving charges:

\begin{formula}[Force on a current]
\[
    \vect{F} = \int I\,d\lvect{s}\times\vect{B} = \int\vect{K}\times\vect{B}\,da = \int\vect{J}\times\vect{B}\,dV .
\]
\end{formula}

\begin{example}[Current loop levitated by a magnetic force]
Equilibrium condition $\vect{F}_g + \vect{F}_B = 0$ with $\vect{F}_g = m\lvect{g} = -mg\,\hat{z}$:

\[
    \vect{F}_B = \int I\,d\lvect{s}\times\vect{B} = \int_0^\ell I\,d\lvect{s}\times B\,\hat{x} = \int_0^\ell I\,dy\,B\,\hat{z} = IB\ell\,\hat{z}
\]

Thus $IB\ell = mg$, giving:
\[
    \vect{I} = \frac{mg}{B\ell}\,\hat{y}
\]
\end{example}


\begin{fact}[Magnetic forces do no work]
\[
    \frac{dW}{dt} = \vect{F}\cdot\lvect{v} = q(\lvect{v}\times\vect{B})\cdot\lvect{v} = 0 .
\]
$\vect{B}$ changes the direction of $\lvect{v}$, never the speed. When a magnetic force appears to lift a current loop (as in the example below), the work is supplied by whatever maintains the current, such as the battery; the magnetic force only redirects it.
\end{fact}

\begin{example}[Wien filter (velocity selector)]
Force balance:
\[
    \vect{F} = q(\vect{E} + \lvect{v}\times\vect{B}) = q(E + vB) = 0
\]
\[
    \Rightarrow \quad E = -vB \quad \Rightarrow \quad v = \frac{E}{B}
\]

The Wien filter balances electric and magnetic forces so that only particles with $v = E/B$ pass straight through. It selects a single speed regardless of the charge or mass of the particle.
\end{example}

\section{Steady Currents}

Charge conservation gives the continuity equation $\partial\rho/\partial t + \div\vect{J} = 0$ (Section~\ref{sec:em-continuity}). Magnetostatics means \emph{steady} currents, $\partial\rho/\partial t = 0$, and hence $\div\vect{J} = 0$: as much current flows out of any region as flows in.

\begin{fact}[A moving point charge is not a steady current]
For $\rho = q\,\delta^3(\lvect{r}-\lvect{r}(t))$ and $\vect{J} = q\lvect{v}\,\delta^3(\lvect{r}-\lvect{r}(t))$, continuity holds, but
\[
    \div\vect{J} = q\lvect{v}\cdot\grad\delta^3(\lvect{r}-\lvect{r}(t)) = -\pdv{\rho}{t}\neq0 .
\]
The density at a fixed point changes as the charge arrives and leaves, so Biot--Savart does \emph{not} give the field of a single moving charge.
\end{fact}

\section{The Biot--Savart Law}

\begin{formula}[Biot--Savart law]
\[
    d\vect{B} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\,\frac{d\lvect{\ell}\times\rhat}{r^2},
    \qquad
    \vect{B}(\lvect{r}) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{\vect{J}(\lvect{r}\,')\times(\lvect{r}-\lvect{r}\,')}{|\lvect{r}-\lvect{r}\,'|^3}\,dV',
\]
with $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \text{N/A}^2$ and $\rhat$ pointing from the current element to the field point.
\end{formula}
Unlike the radial Coulomb field, the field of a current element circles the element.

Write $\lvect{\mathfrak{r}} = \lvect{r}-\lvect{r}\,'$ for the separation vector from source to field point. For line, surface and volume currents, with $I = \lambda v$, $\vect{K} = d\vect{I}/dl_\perp = \sigma\lvect{v}$ and $\vect{J} = d\vect{I}/da_\perp = \rho\lvect{v}$,
\[
    \vect{B}(\lvect{r}) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{I\,d\lvect{l}\,'\times\hat{\lvect{\mathfrak{r}}}}{\mathfrak{r}^2}
    = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{\vect{K}(\lvect{r}\,')\times\hat{\lvect{\mathfrak{r}}}}{\mathfrak{r}^2}\,da'
    = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{\vect{J}(\lvect{r}\,')\times\hat{\lvect{\mathfrak{r}}}}{\mathfrak{r}^2}\,dV' .
\]
Within magnetostatics this is the experimental starting law, playing the role of Coulomb's law: the field equations $\div\vect{B} = 0$ and $\curl\vect{B} = \mu_0\vect{J}$ are derived from it in Section~\ref{sec:em-bs-div-curl}. Logically the reverse direction also works, and Section~\ref{sec:em-bs-from-a} recovers Biot--Savart from the field equations via the vector potential.




\begin{formula}[Relation between $\mu_0$ and $\varepsilon_0$]
\[
    \mu_0 = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0c^2} = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \frac{\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}}{\text{C}^2}.
\]
\end{formula}

\section{Fields from Biot--Savart}

\subsection{Infinite Straight Wire}

For a wire along $z$, each element contributes a field circling the wire, and the integral over the whole wire gives
\[
    \vect{B} = \frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi s}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}},
\]
with $s$ the distance from the wire and direction by the right-hand rule.

\begin{example}[Force between parallel wires]
Wire 1 produces $B_1 = \mu_0I_1/2\pi d$ at wire 2, a distance $d$ away, perpendicular to wire 2. The force on a length $\ell$ of wire 2 is $F = I_2B_1\ell$:
\[
    \frac{F}{\ell} = \frac{\mu_0I_1I_2}{2\pi d},
\]
attractive for parallel currents and repulsive for antiparallel ones.
\end{example}

Every element $d\lvect{\ell}$ of a loop of radius $R$ is perpendicular to the line to a point $P$ on the axis at height $z$, at distance $r=\sqrt{R^2+z^2}$, so $|d\vect{B}| = \mu_0 I\,d\ell/4\pi r^2$. The components perpendicular to the axis cancel around the loop; the axial component carries a factor $R/r$. Integrating $d\ell$ around the loop gives $2\pi R$:
\begin{formula}[Field on the axis of a circular loop]
\[
    \vect{B} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\,\frac{2\pi R}{r^2}\,\frac{R}{r}\,\khat = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2}\,\frac{R^2}{(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\,\khat,
    \qquad
    B_\text{centre} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2R}.
\]
\end{formula}

The same result follows without the symmetry argument by writing everything in Cartesian components:
\[
    \lvect{r}\,' = R(\cos\phi',\sin\phi',0),
    \quad
    d\lvect{l}\,' = R\,d\phi'(-\sin\phi',\cos\phi',0),
    \quad
    \lvect{\mathfrak{r}} = (-R\cos\phi',-R\sin\phi',z),
\]
\[
    d\lvect{l}\,'\times\lvect{\mathfrak{r}} = R\,d\phi'\,(z\cos\phi',\ z\sin\phi',\ R),
\]
and the $x$ and $y$ parts integrate to zero over $0\le\phi'\le2\pi$. Position-dependent unit vectors such as $\uvect{s}$ and $\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}$ must be written in Cartesian components \emph{before} integrating, since they point in different directions at different source points.

\begin{fact}[Far field of a loop]
For $z\gg R$,
\[
    \vect{B} \approx \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{2\lvect{m}}{z^3},
    \qquad
    \lvect{m} = I\pi R^2\,\khat = I\lvect{a},
\]
the same form as the on-axis field of an electric dipole, $E_z = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{2p}{z^3}$, with $1/\varepsilon_0\to\mu_0$ and $\lvect{p}\to\lvect{m}$.
\end{fact}


\subsection{Finite Solenoid, on Axis}

A solenoid with $n$ turns per unit length is a stack of loops; a slice $dz$ carries current $nI\,dz$. Measure the position of each slice by the angle $\theta$ between the axis and the line from the field point to the loop's rim, so that $z = R\cot\theta$ and $dz = -R\,d\theta/\sin^2\theta$, while $R^2/(R^2+z^2)^{3/2} = \sin^3\theta/R$. Then
\[
    B_z = \frac{\mu_0 nI}{2}\int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2}\sin\theta\,d\theta
        = \frac{\mu_0 nI}{2}\bigl(\cos\theta_1-\cos\theta_2\bigr),
\]
where $\theta_1,\theta_2$ are the angles subtended by the two ends.
\begin{formula}[Field on the axis of a solenoid]
\[
    B_z = \frac{\mu_0 nI}{2}\bigl(\cos\theta_1-\cos\theta_2\bigr)
    \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{centre, length }L\ }\;
    \mu_0 nI\,\frac{L}{\sqrt{L^2+4R^2}}
    \;\xrightarrow{\ L\to\infty\ }\;
    \mu_0 nI .
\]
\end{formula}


\section{Amp\`ere's Law}

\begin{formula}[Amp\`ere's law]
\[
    \oint_{\partial S}\vect{B}\cdot d\lvect{l} = \mu_0I_{\text{enc}} = \mu_0\int_S\vect{J}\cdot d\lvect{a}
    \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
    \curl\vect{B} = \mu_0\vect{J},
\]
the two forms related by Stokes' theorem. The sense of the loop and the sign of $I_{\text{enc}}$ are related by the right-hand rule.
\end{formula}

\paragraph{From the infinite wire.} With $\vect{B} = (\mu_0I/2\pi s)\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}$ and $d\lvect{l} = ds\,\uvect{s} + s\,d\phi\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}} + dz\,\khat$,
\[
    \vect{B}\cdot d\lvect{l} = \frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi s}\,s\,d\phi = \frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi}\,d\phi .
\]
The shape of the loop drops out: only the angle swept about the wire matters. A loop that encircles the wire once has $\oint d\phi = 2\pi$; one that does not returns $\phi$ to its starting value, so $\oint d\phi = 0$. By superposition over a bundle of straight wires, $\oint\vect{B}\cdot d\lvect{l} = \mu_0I_{\text{enc}}$.

\subsection{Divergence and Curl of the Biot--Savart Field}
\label{sec:em-bs-div-curl}

Ampère's law holds for any steady current, not just bundles of straight wires: both field equations follow from Biot--Savart. Write $\vect{B} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\vect{J}(\lvect{r}\,')\times(\hat{\lvect{\mathfrak{r}}}/\mathfrak{r}^2)\,dV'$. Here $\grad$ acts on $\lvect{r}$ only, so $\vect{J}(\lvect{r}\,')$ passes through it as a constant.

\paragraph{Divergence.}
\[
    \div\Bigl(\vect{J}\times\frac{\hat{\lvect{\mathfrak{r}}}}{\mathfrak{r}^2}\Bigr)
    = \frac{\hat{\lvect{\mathfrak{r}}}}{\mathfrak{r}^2}\cdot(\curl\vect{J}) - \vect{J}\cdot\Bigl(\curl\frac{\hat{\lvect{\mathfrak{r}}}}{\mathfrak{r}^2}\Bigr) = 0 .
\]
The first term vanishes because $\vect{J}$ depends only on $\lvect{r}\,'$. The second vanishes because $\hat{\lvect{\mathfrak{r}}}/\mathfrak{r}^2 = -\grad(1/\mathfrak{r})$ is a gradient, with no hidden delta function in its curl. Hence $\div\vect{B} = 0$: Biot--Savart fields have no magnetic monopoles.

\paragraph{Curl.}
\[
    \curl\Bigl(\vect{J}\times\frac{\hat{\lvect{\mathfrak{r}}}}{\mathfrak{r}^2}\Bigr)
    = \vect{J}\Bigl(\div\frac{\hat{\lvect{\mathfrak{r}}}}{\mathfrak{r}^2}\Bigr) - (\vect{J}\cdot\grad)\frac{\hat{\lvect{\mathfrak{r}}}}{\mathfrak{r}^2} .
\]
This divergence is \emph{not} zero: $\div(\hat{\lvect{\mathfrak{r}}}/\mathfrak{r}^2) = 4\pi\delta^3(\lvect{r}-\lvect{r}\,')$, so the first term gives $\mu_0\vect{J}(\lvect{r})$. In the second, replace $\grad\to-\grad'$ and use
\[
    (\vect{J}\cdot\grad')f = \grad'\cdot(f\vect{J}) - f\,(\grad'\cdot\vect{J}) .
\]
The last piece vanishes for steady currents, and the total divergence becomes a surface integral over a boundary enclosing all the current, where $\vect{J} = 0$. Hence $\curl\vect{B} = \mu_0\vect{J}$.

\begin{theorem}[Uniqueness]
If $\vect{B}$ vanishes at infinity, then $\div\vect{B} = 0$ and $\curl\vect{B} = \mu_0\vect{J}$ determine $\vect{B}$ uniquely for a given $\vect{J}$. This is a special case of the Helmholtz theorem.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Maxwell's Equations So Far}

\begin{formula}[Maxwell's Equations (magnetostatics)]
\begin{align*}
    \div \vect{E} &= \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0} & \div \vect{B} &= 0 \\
    \curl \vect{E} &= 0 & \curl \vect{B} &= \mu_0 \vect{J}
\end{align*}
\end{formula}


\subsection{Using Amp\`ere's Law}

Like Gauss's law, Amp\`ere's law determines $\vect{B}$ only when symmetry fixes its direction and dependence.

\begin{example}[Infinite straight wire]
A circle of radius $s$ around the wire gives $B\cdot2\pi s = \mu_0I$, so $B = \mu_0I/2\pi s$, as from Biot--Savart.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Solid cylindrical wire]
Inside a wire carrying uniform current density $\vect{J} = J\khat$, a circle of radius $s$ encloses $J\pi s^2$, so $B\cdot2\pi s = \mu_0J\pi s^2$ and
\[
    \vect{B} = \frac{\mu_0Js}{2}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}} = \frac{\mu_0}{2}\,\vect{J}\times\lvect{s},
\]
where $\lvect{s}$ is the perpendicular position vector from the axis. Outside, the wire looks like a line current.
\end{example}

\subsection{Infinite Current Sheet}

A sheet in the plane $y=0$ carries surface current density $K$ (current per unit width) in the $x$-direction. Treat it as many parallel wires: a strip of width $dz$ carries $dI = K\,dz$ and, at distance $r=\sqrt{z^2+y^2}$, produces a field of magnitude $\mu_0\,dI/2\pi r$ circling the strip. The components normal to the sheet cancel between strips at $\pm z$, and the components parallel to the sheet add, with a factor $\cos\theta = |y|/r$:
\[
    |B| = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{\mu_0 K\,dz}{2\pi r}\,\frac{|y|}{r}
        = \frac{\mu_0 K |y|}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{dz}{z^2+y^2}
        = \frac{\mu_0 K}{2}.
\]
Compactly, $\vect{B} = \frac{\mu_0}{2}\,\vect{K}\times\uvect{n}$, with $\uvect{n}$ the unit normal pointing from the sheet toward the field point: here $\vect{B} = \pm\frac{\mu_0K}{2}\,\khat$ above and below.
\begin{formula}[Field of an infinite current sheet]
\[
    |\vect{B}| = \frac{\mu_0 K}{2},
\]
parallel to the sheet and perpendicular to the current, reversing direction across the sheet and independent of the distance from it.
\end{formula}
Crossing the sheet, the parallel component of $\vect{B}$ jumps by $\mu_0K$, the magnetic analogue of the jump $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$ in the normal component of $\vect{E}$ across a sheet of charge.

Amp\`ere's law gives the same result directly: a rectangular loop of length $\ell$ straddling the sheet encloses current $K\ell$, and only its two sides parallel to the sheet contribute, so $2B\ell = \mu_0K\ell$.

\section{The Vector Potential and Gauge}
\label{sec:em-vector-potential}

Since $\div\vect{B}=0$, $\vect{B}$ can always be written as a curl,
\[
    \vect{B} = \curl\vect{A},
\]
which satisfies $\div\vect{B}=\div(\curl\vect{A})=0$ automatically. $\vect{A}$ is the \emph{magnetic vector potential}. For example, the uniform field $\vect{B}=B_0\khat$ is produced by $\vect{A}=B_0x\,\jhat$, or equally by $\vect{A}=-B_0y\,\ihat$, or by $\vect{A}=\tfrac12B_0(-y\,\ihat+x\,\jhat)$.

\subsection{Gauge Freedom}

As the example shows, $\vect{A}$ is not unique. Two potentials give the same $\vect{B}$ if and only if $\curl(\vect{A}-\vect{A}')=0$, i.e.\ $\vect{A}' = \vect{A}+\grad\psi$ for some scalar $\psi$. This freedom can be used to impose the \emph{Coulomb gauge} $\div\vect{A}=0$: if $\div\vect{A}=f\neq0$, choose $\psi$ with $\laplacian\psi=-f$, which is an electrostatics-type Poisson problem and always solvable, and then $\div\vect{A}'=0$.
Explicitly, given $\vect{A}_0$ with $\div\vect{A}_0\neq0$, the gauge function
\[
    \psi(\lvect{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi}\int\frac{\grad'\cdot\vect{A}_0(\lvect{r}\,')}{|\lvect{r}-\lvect{r}\,'|}\,dV'
\]
solves $\laplacian\psi = -\div\vect{A}_0$, so the Coulomb gauge is always reachable.

By Stokes' theorem the line integral of $\vect{A}$ is the magnetic flux, the analogue of Amp\`ere's law with $\vect{B}\to\vect{A}$ and $\mu_0I_{\text{enc}}\to\Phi$:
\[
    \oint\vect{A}\cdot d\lvect{l} = \int\vect{B}\cdot d\lvect{a} = \Phi .
\]

\subsection{The Vector Poisson Equation}

In the Coulomb gauge, Amp\`ere's law $\curl\vect{B}=\mu_0\vect{J}$ becomes
\[
    \curl(\curl\vect{A}) = \grad(\div\vect{A}) - \laplacian\vect{A} = -\laplacian\vect{A} = \mu_0\vect{J}.
\]
\begin{formula}[Poisson equation for the vector potential]
\[
    \laplacian\vect{A} = -\mu_0\vect{J},
    \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad
    \laplacian A_i = -\mu_0 J_i \quad (i=x,y,z),
\]
three copies of the electrostatic Poisson equation $\laplacian\phi=-\rho/\varepsilon_0$.
\end{formula}
Each component is therefore solved exactly as in electrostatics, with $\rho/\varepsilon_0\to\mu_0J_i$:

\begin{formula}[Vector potential of a current distribution]
\[
    \vect{A}(\lvect{r}) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{\vect{J}(\lvect{r}\,')}{|\lvect{r}-\lvect{r}\,'|}\,dV',
    \qquad
    \vect{A}(\lvect{r}) = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\oint\frac{d\lvect{\ell}\,'}{|\lvect{r}-\lvect{r}\,'|}\ \ \text{(thin wire)},
\]
using $\vect{J}\,dV = I\,d\lvect{\ell}$ for a wire.
\end{formula}

This solution is consistent with the gauge condition it assumed only for steady currents. Taking its divergence and integrating by parts, for a localized $\vect{J}$,
\[
    \div\vect{A} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{\grad'\cdot\vect{J}(\lvect{r}\,')}{|\lvect{r}-\lvect{r}\,'|}\,dV' = 0
    \quad\text{since } \grad'\cdot\vect{J} = 0 .
\]

\begin{example}[Long straight wire]
A wire along $x$ carries current $I$. $A_x$ obeys the same equation as the potential of a line charge, with $\lambda/\varepsilon_0\to\mu_0I$, so by analogy with $\phi=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln(a/r)$,
\[
    A_x = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi}\ln\frac{a}{r},
\]
with $r$ the distance from the wire and $a$ an arbitrary reference radius. Its curl gives $|\vect{B}| = |\partial A_x/\partial r| = \mu_0I/2\pi r$, circling the wire, as from Amp\`ere's law.
\end{example}

\subsection{Recovering Biot--Savart}
\label{sec:em-bs-from-a}

For a single current element, $d\vect{A} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\frac{d\lvect{\ell}}{r}$, with $r$ the distance from the element to the field point. Since $d\lvect{\ell}$ is fixed, $\curl(d\lvect{\ell}/r) = \grad(1/r)\times d\lvect{\ell}$, and $\grad(1/r) = -\rhat/r^2$ where $\rhat$ points from the element to the field point. Hence $d\vect{B}=\curl(d\vect{A})$ is
\[
    d\vect{B} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\,\frac{d\lvect{\ell}\times\rhat}{r^2},
    \qquad
    \vect{B}(\lvect{r}) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{\vect{J}(\lvect{r}\,')\times(\lvect{r}-\lvect{r}\,')}{|\lvect{r}-\lvect{r}\,'|^3}\,dV'.
\]

This is Biot--Savart, now derived from the field equations.

\section{The Infinite Solenoid}
\label{sec:em-solenoid}

Take an ideal solenoid of radius $R$, with $n$ turns per unit length carrying current $I$, so that $\vect{K} = nI\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}$. It is symmetric under translations along $z$ and rotations about it, so each component of $\vect{B}$ depends on $s$ alone.
\begin{itemize}
    \item $B_s = 0$: a coaxial Gaussian cylinder has $\oint\vect{B}\cdot d\lvect{a} = B_s\,2\pi sL = 0$, the end caps cancelling.
    \item $B_\phi = 0$: a coaxial circle bounds a disk that no $\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}$-directed current crosses, so $B_\phi\,2\pi s = 0$.
\end{itemize}
So $\vect{B} = B_z(s)\,\khat$ everywhere. (A real helical winding also carries a net current $I$ along $z$, which adds $B_\phi = \mu_0I/2\pi s$ outside. The ideal solenoid drops it.)

Outside, a rectangular loop that no wire pierces has $\oint\vect{B}\cdot d\lvect{l} = 0$. Its radial sides contribute nothing, since $\vect{B}\perp d\lvect{l}$ there, so $B(a)L - B(b)L = 0$: $B$ is uniform outside. It must vanish at infinity, so $B_{\text{out}} = 0$. Inside, an Amp\`erian rectangle of length $L$ with one side inside and one outside (Figure~\ref{fig:em-solenoid}) picks up only its inner side, so $B_{\text{in}}L = \mu_0nIL$:
\[
    \vect{B}_{\text{in}} = \mu_0nI\,\khat,
\]
uniform: the magnetic analogue of the parallel-plate capacitor.

\begin{formula}[Vector potential of an infinite solenoid]
The current extends to infinity, so the integral formula for $\vect{A}$ fails. Instead use $\oint\vect{A}\cdot d\lvect{l} = \Phi$ on coaxial circles, with $\vect{A} = A_\phi(s)\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}$:
\[
    \vect{A}_{\text{in}} = \frac{\mu_0nI}{2}\,s\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}},
    \qquad
    \vect{A}_{\text{out}} = \frac{\mu_0nIR^2}{2s}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}.
\]
\end{formula}
As a check, $(\curl\vect{A})_z = \frac1s\partial_s(sA_\phi)$ --- not $\partial_sA_\phi$, since $\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}$ itself varies --- gives $\mu_0nI$ inside and $0$ outside, and $A_\phi$ is continuous at $s = R$.

\begin{fact}[Aharonov--Bohm effect]
Outside the solenoid $\vect{B} = 0$, yet $\oint\vect{A}\cdot d\lvect{l} = \Phi\neq0$, so $\vect{A}$ cannot be gauged away there. Electrons passing on either side of the solenoid pick up a measurable relative phase $q\Phi/\hbar$, although they never enter a region of nonzero field.
\end{fact}


\section{The Magnetic Multipole Expansion}
\label{sec:em-mag-multipole}

\subsection{Expanding the Vector Potential}

For $r>r'$, with $\alpha$ the angle between $\lvect{r}$ and $\lvect{r}\,'$, the Legendre expansion used for the electric multipoles (Section~\ref{sec:em-multipole}) gives
\[
    \frac{1}{|\lvect{r}-\lvect{r}\,'|} = \frac1r\sum_{n=0}^\infty\left(\frac{r'}{r}\right)^nP_n(\cos\alpha).
\]
Applied to a thin loop,
\[
    \vect{A}(\lvect{r}) = \frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\oint\frac{d\lvect{l}\,'}{|\lvect{r}-\lvect{r}\,'|}
    = \frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\sum_n\frac{1}{r^{n+1}}\oint(r')^nP_n(\cos\alpha)\,d\lvect{l}\,'.
\]

\begin{fact}[No magnetic monopole term]
\[
    \vect{A}_{\text{mono}} = \frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi r}\oint d\lvect{l}\,' = 0 .
\]
The displacements around a closed loop sum to zero. There is no magnetic analogue of the total charge $Q$: steady currents close on themselves ($\div\vect{J} = 0$), equivalently $\div\vect{B} = 0$. For a localized steady volume current, $\int\vect{J}\,dV' = 0$ as well. The dipole term therefore dominates far away, with $A\propto1/r^2$ and $B\propto1/r^3$.
\end{fact}

\subsection{The Dipole Term}

The $n = 1$ term is
\[
    \vect{A}_{\text{dip}} = \frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi r^2}\oint(\rhat\cdot\lvect{r}\,')\,d\lvect{l}\,'.
\]

\begin{theorem}[A corollary of Stokes' theorem]
For any scalar $T$,
\[
    \oint T\,d\lvect{l} = -\int\grad T\times d\lvect{a}.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For a constant vector $\lvect{c}$, Stokes' theorem applied to $\lvect{c}\,T$ gives $\lvect{c}\cdot\oint T\,d\lvect{l} = \int(\grad T\times\lvect{c})\cdot d\lvect{a} = -\lvect{c}\cdot\int\grad T\times d\lvect{a}$. Since $\lvect{c}$ is arbitrary, the vectors agree.
\end{proof}

With $T = \rhat\cdot\lvect{r}\,'$ ($\rhat$ is fixed during the source integration), $\grad'T = \rhat$, so $\oint(\rhat\cdot\lvect{r}\,')\,d\lvect{l}\,' = -\rhat\times\int d\lvect{a}\,' = \lvect{a}\times\rhat$.

\begin{formula}[Magnetic dipole potential]
\[
    \vect{A}_{\text{dip}} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{\lvect{m}\times\rhat}{r^2},
    \qquad
    \lvect{m} = I\lvect{a} = I\int d\lvect{a},
    \qquad
    \lvect{m} = \frac12\int\lvect{r}\times\vect{J}\,dV\ \ \text{(volume current)}.
\]
\end{formula}

The vector area $\lvect{a}$ depends only on the loop, not on the surface chosen: two surfaces with the same boundary form a closed surface, and $\oint d\lvect{a} = 0$. For a flat loop, $\lvect{a}$ is the area times the right-hand normal.

\subsection{The Vector Area as a Line Integral}

The vector area can be written without choosing any surface:

\begin{formula}[Vector area]
\[
    \lvect{a} = \int_S d\lvect{a} = \frac12\oint\lvect{r}\times d\lvect{l}
    \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
    \lvect{m} = \frac I2\oint\lvect{r}\times d\lvect{l}.
\]
\end{formula}

\paragraph{Geometric meaning.} The triangle with vertices at the origin, $\lvect{r}$ and $\lvect{r}+d\lvect{l}$ has vector area $\frac12\lvect{r}\times d\lvect{l}$: half the parallelogram spanned by $\lvect{r}$ and $d\lvect{l}$, with normal by the right-hand rule. Going around the loop, these slivers form a cone from the origin to the curve, a surface bounded by the loop, so their sum is $\lvect{a}$. For a planar loop with the origin in its plane this is the shoelace formula $A = \frac12\oint(x\,dy - y\,dx)$, which is Kepler's equal-areas idea: $|\lvect{r}\times d\lvect{l}|/2$ is the area swept by the position vector.

Shifting the origin, $\lvect{r}\to\lvect{r}+\lvect{c}$, adds $\frac12\lvect{c}\times\oint d\lvect{l} = 0$, so the result is origin-independent. For a non-planar loop, $a_z$ is the area of the loop's projection onto the $xy$-plane, and similarly for the other components.

\begin{proof}
Apply the Stokes corollary to each component $T = x_j$:
\[
    \Bigl(\oint\lvect{r}\times d\lvect{l}\Bigr)_i = \epsilon_{ijk}\oint x_j\,dl_k
    = -\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{kmn}\int\partial_mx_j\,da_n
    = -\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{kjn}\int da_n
    = 2\int da_i . \qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

The form $\frac12\oint\lvect{r}\times I\,d\lvect{l}$ generalizes at once to any current distribution via $I\,d\lvect{l}\to\vect{J}\,dV$, giving the general definition $\lvect{m} = \frac12\int\lvect{r}\times\vect{J}\,dV$.

\begin{example}[Gyromagnetic ratio]
For a charge $q$ of mass $m_e$ in a circular orbit, $\vect{J}\,dV\to q\lvect{v}$ gives
\[
    \lvect{m} = \frac q2\,\lvect{r}\times\lvect{v} = \frac{q}{2m_e}\vect{L}.
\]
\end{example}

\subsection{Field of a Pure Dipole}

Take $\lvect{m} = m\khat$, so $\vect{A}_{\text{dip}} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{m\sin\theta}{r^2}\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}$. The spherical curl of an azimuthal field is
\[
    B_r = \frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\partial_\theta(\sin\theta\,A_\phi),
    \qquad
    B_\theta = -\frac1r\partial_r(rA_\phi).
\]

\begin{formula}[Magnetic dipole field]
\[
    \vect{B}_{\text{dip}} = \frac{\mu_0m}{4\pi r^3}\bigl(2\cos\theta\,\rhat + \sin\theta\,\tthat\bigr)
    = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{3(\lvect{m}\cdot\rhat)\rhat - \lvect{m}}{r^3},
\]
identical in form to the electric dipole field with $\lvect{p}/\varepsilon_0\to\mu_0\lvect{m}$.
\end{formula}

On the axis ($\theta = 0$), $B_z = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{2m}{z^3}$ with $m = I\pi R^2$ for a ring: the far-field limit of the exact on-axis field of a loop found earlier.

\begin{fact}[Where the dipole formula holds]
The expansion assumes that all the current lies closer to the origin than the field point ($r'<r$). It therefore describes the field \emph{outside} a source, never inside. If the current has a single angular mode with $\ell = 1$ (for example $\vect{J}\propto\sin\theta\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}$, as on a spinning charged sphere), every other multipole vanishes and the dipole field is \emph{exact} outside.
\end{fact}

\section{Magnetostatic Boundary Conditions}
\label{sec:em-mag-bc}

Let a surface carry current $\vect{K}$, with normal $\uvect{n}$ pointing from ``below'' to ``above''. Pillbox and thin-loop arguments go through as in electrostatics: as the height $\epsilon\to0$, the side contributions (proportional to $\epsilon$ times a finite field) vanish, and only sources concentrated on the surface survive. The pillbox and $\div\vect{B} = 0$ give a continuous normal component; a thin loop and Amp\`ere's law give a jump $\mu_0K$ in the tangential component perpendicular to $\vect{K}$.

\begin{formula}[Boundary conditions on $\vect{B}$ and $\vect{A}$]
\[
    B^\perp_{\text{above}} = B^\perp_{\text{below}},
    \qquad
    \vect{B}_{\text{above}} - \vect{B}_{\text{below}} = \mu_0\,\vect{K}\times\uvect{n},
\]
\[
    \vect{A}_{\text{above}} = \vect{A}_{\text{below}},
    \qquad
    \pdv{\vect{A}_{\text{above}}}{n} - \pdv{\vect{A}_{\text{below}}}{n} = -\mu_0\vect{K}.
\]
\end{formula}

\begin{example}[Solenoid check]
At $s = R$, $\uvect{n} = \uvect{s}$ and $\vect{K}\times\uvect{n} = nI\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\times\uvect{s} = -nI\,\khat$, so $\vect{B}_{\text{out}} - \vect{B}_{\text{in}} = -\mu_0nI\,\khat$, as found in Section~\ref{sec:em-solenoid}. Likewise $\partial_sA^{\text{out}}_\phi - \partial_sA^{\text{in}}_\phi = -\mu_0nI$.
\end{example}

In the Hall effect, a transverse magnetic field deflects current carriers to one side of the conductor, building up a transverse electric field that balances the magnetic force. In steady state the electric force exactly cancels the magnetic force:
\[
    eE_H = ev B \quad \Rightarrow \quad E_H = vB.
\]
The current density $\vect{J} = -ne\lvect{v}$ gives the drift speed $v = J/(ne) = I/(neh\ell)$, and the Hall voltage $V_H = E_H \ell$ becomes:
\[
    V_H = \left(\frac{B}{neh}\right) I.
\]
The sign of the Hall voltage reveals the sign of the charge carriers, and its magnitude measures the carrier density $n$.


\chapter{Electromagnetic Induction}

\section{Conducting Rod in a Magnetic Field}

The charges inside the moving rod experience a force $\vect{F} = q\lvect{v}\times\vect{B}$ (not a force from $\vect{E}$). This creates a charge separation (positive charges accumulate at one end).

\subsection{Electromotive Force (EMF)}

\begin{definition}[Electromotive Force]
\[
    \mathcal{E} \equiv \frac{1}{q}\oint \lvect{f}\cdot d\lvect{s}
\]
The EMF is the electromotive force (not actually a force --- it has units of electric potential). It is any influence that causes charge to circulate around a closed path. EMF must be non-electrostatic, since $\curl\vect{E} = 0$ for electrostatic fields and a conservative field cannot drive a current around a closed loop.
\end{definition}

Electromagnetic induction arises when the magnetic flux through a circuit changes, whether by moving the circuit, changing the current in a nearby circuit, or changing $\vect{B}$ in time. The induced EMF is $\mathcal{E} = -d\Phi_B/dt$ (Faraday's law).

\begin{fact}[Useful Unit Conversion]
\[
    \frac{1\,\text{m}^2 \cdot \text{Tesla}}{\text{second}} = 1\,\text{Volt}
\]
\end{fact}
