\chapter{Conductors and Capacitance}

A conductor contains charges free to move in response to any field. That, plus the demand that nothing move, fixes the interior field and charge density, the field at the surface, and the potential throughout. We derive each, then develop capacitance for one, two, and many conductors.

% =====================================================================
\section{Electrostatic Equilibrium}
% =====================================================================

Throughout, the conductor is in \emph{electrostatic equilibrium}: nothing moves and all quantities are time-independent. Everything below follows from this alone, whatever the conductor's shape or surroundings.

\begin{theorem}[The interior field vanishes]
$\vect{E} = 0$ at every point in the interior of a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If $\vect{E}\neq 0$ at an interior point, the free charges there feel $\vect{F}=q\vect{E}\neq 0$ and accelerate, contradicting equilibrium.
\end{proof}

This concerns the \emph{full} vector, not just one component --- a point used below. It says nothing about the field outside: the free charges redistribute until their field cancels any applied field inside. An insulator cannot do this, so an external field penetrates it essentially unchanged.

The vanishing of the interior field immediately constrains where charge can sit.

\begin{theorem}[Charge resides on the surface]
The volume charge density $\rho$ vanishes throughout the interior of a conductor, and any excess charge resides entirely on its surface.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Gauss's law gives $\rho = \varepsilon_0\div\vect{E} = 0$ at every interior point, so any net charge must sit on the boundary as a surface density $\sigma$.
\end{proof}

Macroscopically $\sigma$ is a two-dimensional sheet; its atomic-scale thickness plays no role below.

\section{The Field at the Surface}
\label{sec:em-surface}

The field just outside is a vector, so its normal and tangential components must be fixed separately; the pillbox below settles only the first.

Take a pillbox straddling the surface, with faces of area $A$ just inside and just outside and a side wall of negligible height. Gauss's law gives
\[
    \oint_S \vect{E}\cdot\hat{n}\,da = \frac{Q_\text{enc}}{\varepsilon_0}.
\]
The inner face contributes nothing and $Q_\text{enc}=\sigma A$, so
\[
    E_\perp A = \frac{\sigma A}{\varepsilon_0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad E_\perp = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}.
\]
A tangential component contributes no flux through either face, so the pillbox is silent about it.

Instead take a thin rectangular loop straddling the surface, with long sides $L$ just outside and just inside and short sides $h\to 0$. Since $\vect{E}$ is curl-free,
\[
    \oint \vect{E}\cdot d\vect{\ell} = 0
\]
around the loop. The short sides vanish as $h\to 0$, and the long sides are traversed oppositely:
\[
    E_{\parallel,\text{out}}\,L - E_{\parallel,\text{in}}\,L = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad E_{\parallel,\text{out}} = E_{\parallel,\text{in}}.
\]
Since the full interior vector vanishes, $E_{\parallel,\text{in}} = 0$ and hence $E_{\parallel,\text{out}} = 0$.

\subsection{The Combined Result}

\begin{formula}[Field just outside a conductor]
\[
    \vect{E}_\text{just outside} = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}\,\hat{n},
\]
normal to the surface. Equivalently, $\sigma = -\varepsilon_0\,\pdv*{\phi}{n}$ with $\pdv*{}{n}$ the outward normal derivative.
\end{formula}

Field lines therefore meet a conductor at right angles --- consistent with equilibrium, since a tangential component would make surface charges slide.

% =====================================================================
\section{The Conductor as an Equipotential}
% =====================================================================

\begin{theorem}[A conductor is an equipotential]
\label{thm:em-equipotential}
The scalar potential $\phi$ is constant throughout a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium --- both its solid interior and its entire surface, including the walls of any cavity carved into it.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Recall the line-integral relation between $\vect{E}$ and $\phi$: for any path $\gamma$ running from a point $P$ to a point $Q$,
\[
    \phi(Q) - \phi(P) = -\int_\gamma \vect{E}\cdot d\vect{\ell}.
\]

\textbf{Step 1 (interior).} Join any two interior points $P$, $Q$ by a path $\gamma$ lying inside the conductor (Figure~\ref{fig:em-equip-interior}). Since $\vect{E}=0$ everywhere along it,
\[
    \phi(Q) - \phi(P) = -\int_\gamma 0\,d\ell = 0 .
\]
Since $P$ and $Q$ were arbitrary, $\phi \equiv \phi_0$ for a single constant $\phi_0$ throughout the interior.

  \filldraw (-1.0,-0.2) circle (1.5pt) node[above, font=\small] {$P$};
  \filldraw (0.9,0.7) circle (1.5pt) node[above right, font=\small] {$Q$};
  \draw[->, dashed] (-1.0,-0.2) to[out=40,in=200] (0.9,0.7);

\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\caption{Step 1. Any two interior points are joined by a path lying wholly inside the conductor, along which the integrand vanishes identically, so all interior points share one potential $\phi_0$.}
\label{fig:em-equip-interior}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Step 2 (continuity across the surface).} Join $P_\text{below}$ just inside to $P_\text{above}$ just outside by a normal path of length $h$ (Figure~\ref{fig:em-equip-continuity}):
\[
    \bigl|\phi(P_\text{above}) - \phi(P_\text{below})\bigr|
    = \left|\int_{P_\text{below}}^{P_\text{above}} \vect{E}\cdot d\vect{\ell}\right|
    \le \Bigl(\max_{\text{path}}|\vect{E}|\Bigr)\, h .
\]
Since $\sigma$, and hence $\vect{E}$, stays finite, the bound vanishes as $h\to 0$: $\phi$ is continuous across the surface.

\label{fig:em-equip-continuity}
\end{figure}

Finiteness of $\sigma$ is essential: for an idealized \emph{dipole layer} ($\sigma\to\infty$ as thickness $d\to 0$ with $\sigma d$ fixed) the bound does not vanish and $\phi$ genuinely jumps.

\textbf{Step 3 (surface).} For a surface point $R$, take interior points $P_n\to R$ (Figure~\ref{fig:em-equip-surface}); each has $\phi(P_n)=\phi_0$, so by Step 2
\[
    \phi(R) = \lim_{n\to\infty}\phi(P_n) = \lim_{n\to\infty}\phi_0 = \phi_0 .
\]
This holds for every surface point, including cavity walls, so $\phi=\phi_0$ throughout the conductor.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Shielding: No Field Lines Within a Conductor}
\label{sec:em-shielding}

Consequently no field line runs through a conductor's material: it would join two points of the conductor with $\phi(B)-\phi(A) = -\int_A^B\vect{E}\cdot d\vect{\ell}\neq 0$. This is the basis of \textbf{electrical shielding}. Shielding of a \emph{cavity}, where there is no material, needs uniqueness and is proved below.

% =====================================================================
\section{Field Lines Between Conductors at Different Potentials}
% =====================================================================

Let conductors $A$, $B$ have $\phi_A>\phi_B$, and follow a field line from $P$ on $A$ to $Q$ on $B$:
\[
    \phi_B - \phi_A = \phi(Q) - \phi(P) = -\int_P^Q \vect{E}\cdot d\vect{\ell} < 0 .
\]
So $\vect{E}\cdot d\vect{\ell}$ is positive on average along the path: $\vect{E}$ points from $A$ toward $B$.

\begin{fact}
Field lines point from the higher-potential conductor toward the lower-potential one.
\end{fact}

This recovers the familiar statement that positive charges are pushed from high to low potential, now as a consequence rather than an assumption.

\section{Summary of Conductor Properties}
% =====================================================================

\begin{formula}[Conductor properties in electrostatic equilibrium]
For any conductor in equilibrium, whatever its shape and whatever external charges are present:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $\vect{E} = 0$ everywhere in the material.
    \item $\rho = 0$ in the material; all net charge sits on the surface as $\sigma$.
    \item $\phi$ is constant over the material and its surface: $\phi_a = \phi_b$ for any two points of one conductor.
    \item Just outside, $\vect{E} = (\sigma/\varepsilon_0)\,\hat{n}$ --- perpendicular to the surface, with no tangential part --- so that $\sigma = -\varepsilon_0\,\pdv*{\phi}{n}$ and $Q_\text{enc} = \oint\sigma\,da = \varepsilon_0\oint\vect{E}\cdot d\lvect{a}$.
    \item No field line passes through the material, which is what makes a conductor a shield.
\end{enumerate}
\end{formula}

\section{The Boundary-Value Problem and Uniqueness}
% =====================================================================

Since $\phi$ is a scalar it is easier to work with than $\vect{E}$. We seek $\phi$ with $\laplacian\phi = -\rho/\varepsilon_0$ subject to conditions on the bounding conductors.

\label{fig:em-bvp-setup}
\end{figure}

Precisely, such a \textbf{boundary-value problem} is specified by the following data (Figure~\ref{fig:em-bvp-setup}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item the shapes and positions of the conducting surfaces $S_1,\dots,S_n$;
    \item any prescribed free charge density $\rho(\lvect{x})$ in the region outside them;
    \item for \emph{each} conductor, exactly one of its potential $\phi_i$ or its total charge $Q_i$ --- never both, which would be over-determined, nor neither, which would leave the problem unsolved;
    \item for an unbounded region, $\phi\to 0$ as $|\lvect{x}|\to\infty$.
\end{itemize}
Solving means finding $\phi$ (hence $\vect{E}$), the surface charges $\sigma_i = -\varepsilon_0\,\pdv*{\phi}{n}$, and whichever of $\{\phi_i\}$, $\{Q_i\}$ was not given. If $Q_i$ is given, $\phi$ is an \emph{unknown} constant on $S_i$ fixed by $Q_i = -\varepsilon_0\oint_{S_i}\pdv*{\phi}{n}\,da$.

First, the two kinds of boundary condition.

\begin{definition}[Dirichlet boundary condition]
The value of the potential itself is specified at every point of the boundary surface $S = \partial V$:
\[
    \phi(\lvect{x}) = f(\lvect{x}) \quad\text{for } \lvect{x}\in S,
\]
for some prescribed function $f$. Holding each conductor at a given potential is a Dirichlet condition.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Neumann boundary condition]
The normal derivative of the potential --- equivalently the normal component of the field, since $E_n = -\pdv*{\phi}{n}$ --- is specified at every point of $S$:
\[
    \pdv{\phi}{n}(\lvect{x}) = g(\lvect{x}) \quad\text{for } \lvect{x}\in S,
\]
where $\pdv*{}{n} \equiv \hat{n}\cdot\grad$ is the derivative along the outward normal. Specifying the surface charge on each conductor is a Neumann condition, since $\sigma = -\varepsilon_0\,\pdv*{\phi}{n}$.
\end{definition}

Not every function $g$ is admissible Neumann data, however. Integrating Gauss's law over $V$ and applying the divergence theorem gives
\[
    \oint_S \vect{E}\cdot\hat{n}\,da = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\int_V \rho\,d^3x
    \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
    \oint_S \pdv{\phi}{n}\,da = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\int_V \rho\,d^3x .
\]

\begin{fact}[Neumann data cannot be prescribed arbitrarily]
The function $g = \pdv*{\phi}{n}$ cannot be chosen freely on $S$: its integral over the whole boundary is fixed by the total charge enclosed. A proposed Neumann condition violating this identity corresponds to no physical configuration at all.
\end{fact}

\begin{fact}[Cauchy data is over-determined]
Specifying both $\phi$ and $\pdv*{\phi}{n}$ on a closed surface over-determines the problem: by the theorem below Dirichlet data alone fixes the solution, so extra Neumann data is either redundant or inconsistent.
\end{fact}

\begin{theorem}[Uniqueness]
\label{thm:em-uniqueness}
Let $V$ be a region with boundary $S = \partial V$, and let $\phi_1$ and $\phi_2$ both solve Poisson's equation in $V$ for the same charge density,
\[
    \laplacian\phi_1 = \laplacian\phi_2 = -\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0} \quad\text{in } V .
\]
If they satisfy the same boundary condition on $S$ --- either
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Dirichlet, $\phi_1 = \phi_2$ on $S$;
    \item Neumann, $\pdv*{\phi_1}{n} = \pdv*{\phi_2}{n}$ on $S$; or
    \item mixed, Dirichlet on one part of $S$ and Neumann on the rest
\end{enumerate}
--- then $\phi_1 = \phi_2$ throughout $V$ in cases (a) and (c), and $\phi_1 = \phi_2 + \text{const.}$ throughout $V$ in case (b).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $U \equiv \phi_1 - \phi_2$. Since both solve Poisson's equation for the same $\rho$, the sources cancel:
\[
    \laplacian U = \left(-\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}\right) - \left(-\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}\right) = 0 \quad\text{in } V,
\]
so $U$ is harmonic in $V$. Apply Green's first identity (Formula~\ref{form:em-green1}) with both functions equal to $U$:
\[
    \int_V \left(U\laplacian U + |\grad U|^2\right) d^3x = \oint_S U\,\pdv{U}{n}\,da .
\]
The first term vanishes because $U$ is harmonic, leaving
\[
    \int_V |\grad U|^2\,d^3x = \oint_S U\,\pdv{U}{n}\,da .
\]

\emph{The surface term vanishes.} In case (a) $U=0$ on $S$; in (b) $\pdv*{U}{n}=0$; in (c) one or the other holds at each point. In all cases
\[
    \oint_S U\,\pdv{U}{n}\,da = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \int_V |\grad U|^2\,d^3x = 0 .
\]

\emph{Conclusion.} The integrand $|\grad U|^2$ is non-negative pointwise, and a non-negative integrand integrating to zero must vanish identically. Hence $\grad U = 0$ everywhere in $V$, and $U$ is constant throughout.

In cases (a) and (c) $U$ vanishes somewhere on $S$, so the constant is zero and $\phi_1=\phi_2$. In case (b) nothing fixes the constant, so the solutions agree up to an additive constant --- harmless, since $\vect{E}=-\grad\phi$ is unchanged.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The Case of Prescribed Charges}

Specifying a conductor's charge $Q_i$ is \emph{not} case (b): Neumann data fixes $\pdv*{\phi}{n}$ pointwise, whereas $Q_i$ only says $\phi$ is an unknown constant on $S_i$ with $\oint_{S_i}\pdv*{\phi}{n}\,da = -Q_i/\varepsilon_0$. This weaker condition needs its own argument.

\begin{theorem}[Uniqueness with charges prescribed]
\label{thm:em-uniqueness-charges}
Let $V$ be the region exterior to conductors $S_1,\dots,S_n$, and let $\phi_1,\phi_2$ solve Poisson's equation in $V$ for the same $\rho$, vanishing at infinity. If they give every conductor the same charge $Q_i$, then $\phi_1=\phi_2$ throughout $V$, including on every conductor.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $U \equiv \phi_1 - \phi_2$, harmonic in $V$ as before. Because each $\phi_1,\phi_2$ is constant over every conductor, so is $U$: write $U = c_i$ on $S_i$, a priori a different constant for each $i$. Matching charges on $S_i$ gives
\[
    \oint_{S_i} \pdv{U}{n}\,da = \oint_{S_i}\pdv{\phi_1}{n}\,da - \oint_{S_i}\pdv{\phi_2}{n}\,da
    = -\frac{Q_i}{\varepsilon_0} - \left(-\frac{Q_i}{\varepsilon_0}\right) = 0 .
\]
Apply Green's first identity on $V$, whose boundary consists of the surfaces $S_1,\dots,S_n$ together with a sphere at infinity:
\[
    \int_V |\grad U|^2\,d^3x = \sum_{i=1}^n \oint_{S_i} U\,\pdv{U}{n}\,da \;+\; \oint_{\infty} U\,\pdv{U}{n}\,da .
\]
The sphere at infinity contributes nothing, since $U\sim 1/|\lvect{x}|$ and $\pdv*{U}{n}\sim 1/|\lvect{x}|^2$ while the area grows only as $|\lvect{x}|^2$. On each $S_i$, $U=c_i$ factors out, leaving $c_i\oint_{S_i}\pdv*{U}{n}\,da=0$. Hence
\[
    \int_V |\grad U|^2\,d^3x = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \grad U = 0 \text{ everywhere in } V,
\]
so $U$ is constant on the connected region $V$, and since $U\to 0$ at infinity, $U\equiv 0$: the solutions agree everywhere, including every conductor's potential.
\end{proof}

So prescribing charges fixes the solution completely, with no leftover constant: the decay condition at infinity rules out the uniform shift that pure Neumann data on a bounded region allows.

The upshot: any guess --- by images, symmetry, or otherwise --- that satisfies Laplace's equation and every boundary condition is \emph{the} solution. The rest of the chapter rests on this.

The first application completes Section~\ref{sec:em-shielding}: a conductor also shields any cavity hollowed out of it.

\begin{theorem}[Shielding theorem: the Faraday cage]
\label{thm:em-faraday-cage}
Let a conductor of any shape contain an empty cavity $V_\text{cav}$ of any shape --- no charge placed inside it --- and suppose an arbitrary external charge distribution or applied field is present outside the conductor. Then
\[
    \vect{E} = 0 \quad\text{everywhere in } V_\text{cav}, \qquad \sigma = 0 \quad\text{everywhere on the cavity wall}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The equipotential argument (Theorem~\ref{thm:em-equipotential}) never referred to the exterior, so the whole conductor, including the cavity wall $S$, sits at one potential $\phi_0$.

In the charge-free cavity $\laplacian\phi = 0$ with $\phi=\phi_0$ on $S$. The constant $\phi\equiv\phi_0$ satisfies both, so by uniqueness (Theorem~\ref{thm:em-uniqueness}) it is the solution:
\[
    \vect{E} = -\grad\phi_0 = 0 \quad\text{everywhere in } V_\text{cav}.
\]
Then $E_\perp = \sigma/\varepsilon_0$ (Section~\ref{sec:em-surface}) gives $\sigma=0$ on the cavity wall.
\end{proof}

A real Faraday cage --- a mesh rather than a solid shell --- only approximates this result, departing from it to the extent that field lines can thread the gaps.

The theorem assumed the cavity was empty. It is worth asking what survives if it is not.

\begin{theorem}[Corollary: the exterior field is blind to how the enclosed charge is arranged]
\label{thm:em-cavity-charge}
Let a conductor with charge $Q_\text{cond}$, in any external field, contain a cavity holding total charge $Q$. Then the field outside the conductor depends on the cavity's contents only through $Q$ --- not on the charge's position, shape, or distribution.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
A Gaussian surface inside the metal enclosing the cavity shows the cavity wall carries exactly $-Q$, whatever the arrangement; the outer surface therefore carries $Q_\text{cond}+Q$.

Outside, $\phi$ is an unknown constant on the outer surface (Theorem~\ref{thm:em-equipotential}), which carries total charge $Q_\text{cond}+Q$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:em-uniqueness-charges} this data --- which involves the cavity only through $Q$ --- fixes the exterior solution.
\end{proof}

This is weaker than saying the exterior field is that of the enclosed charge alone: the outer charge still distributes according to the outer shape and any external field. The two coincide only in special cases, such as the spherical shell of Example~\ref{ex:em-shell}, where the outer charge spreads uniformly and so mimics a point charge at the center.

Once uniqueness is available these results take little work --- the same licence the method of images exploits next.

\section{The Method of Images}

The method of images applies uniqueness directly: replace a conductor by fictitious \emph{image} charges that reproduce its boundary condition; the resulting field in the physical region is then the correct one.

Place an image charge $-q$ at depth $h$ below the plane. Every point of the plane is equidistant from the two charges, so $\phi=0$ there, and the pair solves Laplace's equation in $z>0$. By uniqueness, the field above the plane is that of the two charges.

\subsection{The Induced Surface Charge}

At distance $r$ from the foot of the charge the horizontal components cancel, leaving
\[
    E_z = -\frac{2q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,(r^2+h^2)}\cdot\frac{h}{\sqrt{r^2+h^2}} = -\frac{qh}{2\pi\varepsilon_0\,(r^2+h^2)^{3/2}} .
\]
With $E_z = \sigma/\varepsilon_0$ just above the plane (Section~\ref{sec:em-surface}),
\[
    \sigma(r) = -\frac{qh}{2\pi\,(r^2+h^2)^{3/2}} .
\]
The induced charge is densest directly beneath the point charge and falls off as $r^{-3}$ far away. Integrating it over the plane, with the area element $2\pi r\,dr$,
\[
    Q_\text{induced} = \int_0^\infty \sigma(r)\,2\pi r\,dr
    = -qh\int_0^\infty \frac{r\,dr}{(r^2+h^2)^{3/2}}
    = -qh\left[\frac{-1}{\sqrt{r^2+h^2}}\right]_0^\infty = -q ,
\]
so the plane draws up exactly the charge of the image it is standing in for --- a satisfying check that the construction is doing what we asked of it.

\subsection{General Strategy}

Conversely, ``copper-plating'' any equipotential of a known solution leaves the field unchanged, since the conductor imposes the boundary condition that surface already satisfied. The method of images runs this backwards.

% =====================================================================
\section{Capacitance}
% =====================================================================

An isolated conductor with charge $Q$ has potential $\phi_0$ relative to infinity. Scaling $Q$ by $\alpha$ scales the boundary data, and $\alpha\phi$ solves the scaled problem, so by uniqueness $Q\propto\phi_0$ with a geometry-dependent constant.

\begin{formula}[Capacitance (isolated conductor)]
\[
    Q = C\phi_0
\]
\[
    [C] = \frac{\text{Coulomb}}{\text{Volt}} = 1\,\text{Farad}
\]
($\epsilon_0$ can be expressed as Farads/metre.)
\end{formula}

An isolated sphere of radius $a$ has $\phi_0 = Q/4\pi\varepsilon_0 a$, so $C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 a$; a disk of the same radius has $C = 8\varepsilon_0 a$.

\subsection{Two Conductors}

For a pair of conductors the useful quantity is referred not to a potential measured from infinity but to the potential \emph{difference} between them.

\begin{definition}[Capacitor]
A \emph{capacitor} is a pair of conductors, separated by insulation, carrying equal and opposite charges $+Q$ and $-Q$, so that every field line leaving one terminates on the other. Its capacitance is
\[
    C = \frac{Q}{|\phi_1 - \phi_2|},
\]
with $Q$ the charge transferred from one conductor to the other, both having started neutral.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Parallel-Plate Capacitor]
Two flat conducting plates at separation $s$, each of area $A$.

\begin{example}[Spherical Capacitor]
Concentric spherical shells of radii $a$ and $b>a$, carrying $Q_a$ and $Q_b$. Outside everything, the potential is that of the total charge,
\[
  \phi_\text{out} = \frac{Q_a + Q_b}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 b},
\]
while on the inner shell the two contributions add, each evaluated at its own radius:
\[
  \phi_a = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{Q_a}{a} + \frac{Q_b}{b}\right).
\]
The outer shell's charge contributes the same amount to both, so it drops out of the difference, and with $Q_b = -Q_a$ on the facing surfaces,
\[
  V = \phi_a - \phi_b = \frac{Q_a}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{1}{a} - \frac{1}{b}\right)
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  C = \frac{4\pi\varepsilon_0}{\dfrac{1}{a} - \dfrac{1}{b}} = 4\pi\varepsilon_0\,\frac{ab}{b-a}.
\]
Letting $b\to\infty$ recovers the isolated sphere, $C\to 4\pi\varepsilon_0 a$, as it should.
\end{example}

\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Two things must be checked.

\emph{(i) It solves Laplace's equation.} Each $\phi^{(k)}$ is harmonic and $\laplacian$ is linear, so
\[
    \laplacian\phi_\text{total} = \sum_k V_k\,\laplacian\phi^{(k)} = 0 .
\]

\emph{(ii) It matches the prescribed boundary data.} On the surface $S_i$ of conductor $i$, each elementary solution satisfies $\phi^{(k)}\big|_{S_i} = \delta_{ik}$ by construction. Hence
\[
    \phi_\text{total}\Big|_{S_i} = \sum_k V_k\,\phi^{(k)}\Big|_{S_i} = V_i\cdot 1 + \sum_{k\neq i} V_k\cdot 0 = V_i .
\]

Being harmonic in the exterior region and matching the prescribed potential on every conductor's surface, $\phi_\text{total}$ is by uniqueness \emph{the} solution; no other candidate is consistent with this boundary data.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The Capacitance Matrix}

The surface charge density on a conductor is $\sigma = -\varepsilon_0\,\pdv*{\phi}{n}$, so the total charge on conductor $i$ is
\[
    Q_i = \oint_{S_i}\sigma\,da = -\varepsilon_0\int_{S_i}\pdv{\phi_\text{total}}{n}\,da .
\]
Substituting the superposition result and using linearity of the integral,
\[
    Q_i = -\varepsilon_0\int_{S_i}\sum_k V_k\,\pdv{\phi^{(k)}}{n}\,da
        = \sum_k V_k\underbrace{\left(-\varepsilon_0\int_{S_i}\pdv{\phi^{(k)}}{n}\,da\right)}_{\displaystyle \equiv\; C_{ik}} .
\]

\begin{formula}[Capacitance matrix]
\[
    Q_i = \sum_{k=1}^n C_{ik}\,V_k .
\]
Each coefficient $C_{ik}$ depends only on the geometry of the conductor system, never on the applied voltages, since it was defined from a problem in which $V_k$ was fixed at exactly $1$ and all other conductors grounded.
\end{formula}

This generalizes $Q=C\phi$: fixing all the potentials fixes every charge, linearly.

\emph{Check.} Scaling all $V_j$ by $\alpha$ scales $\phi$ and every $Q_i$ by $\alpha$, consistent with the formula.

\section{Energy Stored in a Capacitor}
% =====================================================================

\[
  W = \frac{1}{C}\int_0^{Q_f} Q\,dQ = \frac{Q_f^2}{2C} \;\defeq\; U_C
  \qquad \text{-- Energy stored in a Capacitor}
\]

The energy can be written three equivalent ways:

\begin{formula}[Energy stored in a capacitor]
\[
  U_C = \frac{Q^2}{2C} = \frac{Q\Phi}{2} = \frac{1}{2}C\Phi^2
\]
\end{formula}

\noindent For a parallel-plate capacitor with area $A$ and separation $s$:

\[
  C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{s}
  \implies
  U_C = \frac{1}{2}\!\left(\frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{s}\right)\!(E\,s)^2
       = \frac{\varepsilon_0 E^2}{2}\cdot \text{Volume}
\]
where $E = \Phi/s$.

\medskip
\noindent This equation also applies to an \emph{isolated charged conductor}. For an isolated sphere:
\[
  C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 a, \qquad
  U_C = \frac{1}{2}Cq^2 \;=\; \frac{1}{2}(4\pi\varepsilon_0 a)\Phi^2
      = \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 a}
\]
which agrees with the earlier calculation for energy stored in the electric field of a charged sphere.

\subsection{Force Required to Keep Capacitor Plates Apart}
%%─────────────────────────────────────────────

If $C(x)$ depends on plate separation $x$, the plates attract and an external force is needed to hold them apart. At constant $Q$, the work done increasing $x$ by $\Delta x$ equals the change in stored energy:

\begin{align*}
  F_\text{ext}\,\Delta x &= \Delta U = \frac{Q^2}{2}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\!\left(\frac{1}{C}\right)\Delta x \\[4pt]
  \implies\quad F_\text{ext} &= \frac{Q^2}{2}\,\frac{d}{dx}\!\left(\frac{1}{C}\right)
\end{align*}

\subsection{Energy Stored per Unit Length (Coaxial Capacitor)}

For the coaxial capacitor above, with $Q = \lambda L$ and $C/L = 2\pi\varepsilon_0/\ln(b/a)$,
\[
  \frac{U}{L} = \frac{1}{L}\cdot\frac{Q^2}{2C} = \frac{\lambda^2}{2}\cdot\frac{L}{C} = \frac{\lambda^2\ln(b/a)}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} .
\]

\subsection{Capacitor Paradox}
%%─────────────────────────────────────────────

Does connecting two capacitors in this configuration leave the field unchanged (A), or confine it between the inner plates (B)? The answer is B: the connected plates must reach a common potential, and assuming nothing changes ignores this.

\medskip\noindent Note: the end state is one of equilibrium, so every result of this chapter applies to it --- in particular each connected pair is a single conductor, at a single potential throughout.
