\section{Conductors in an Electrostatic Field}

In homogeneous isotropic conducting materials free charges redistribute until the internal field vanishes: $\vect{E}=0$ inside, so $\phi$ is constant throughout the conductor and the interior charge density $\rho=0$ by Gauss's law. The field is not zero outside, however. By contrast, in insulators the field penetrates unchanged.

Since $\vect{E}=0$ inside, the conductor is an equipotential volume. The field just outside must be perpendicular to the surface --- any tangential component would drive surface currents, contradicting the static assumption. By Gauss's law applied to a pillbox straddling the surface, $E_\perp = \sigma/\varepsilon_0$ just outside.

\subsection{Summary of Conductor Facts}

The following properties hold for any conductor in electrostatic equilibrium, regardless of its shape or the external charges present.

\begin{formula}[Conductor Properties]
Inside the material of a conductor $\vect{E} = 0$, and likewise $\rho = 0$ there \emph{(only applies when there is no charge)}. All points inside the material of the same conductor share one potential, $\phi_a = \phi_b$ \emph{(since $\phi$ is constant at the boundary and thus forms an equipotential)}. At any point just outside the conductor $\vect{E}$ is perpendicular to the surface with $E = \dfrac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0}$, so the charge enclosed by any surface drawn around it is $Q_\text{enc} = \oint\sigma\,da = \epsilon_0\oint\vect{E}\cdot d\vect{a}$. Any net charge therefore sits on the surface, where it distributes itself \emph{(it is positive, wants to run away, resisting holding itself together)}.
\end{formula}

\begin{example}[Spherically Symmetric Field --- Neutral Conducting Spherical Shell]
Consider a neutral conducting spherical shell enclosing a point charge $q$. A Gaussian surface drawn inside the shell material encloses zero flux, so zero net charge; since the enclosed charge $q$ is already there, the inner surface must carry $-q$. The outer surface then carries $+q$ to keep the conductor neutral overall. The external field is purely radial, identical to that of a point charge $+q$ at the center --- even if $q$ is off-center inside.
\end{example}

\section{General Electrostatic Problem \& Uniqueness Theorem}

Because $\phi$ is a scalar it is easier to work with than $\vect{E}$. We seek $\phi$ satisfying $\laplacian\phi = 0$ (or $-\rho/\varepsilon_0$ where charge is present) subject to boundary conditions on the conducting surfaces that bound the region. Such a \textbf{boundary-value problem} is fully specified either by prescribing $\phi$ on each conductor, or equivalently by specifying the total charge $Q_k$ on each one.

\begin{theorem}[3.1 (Uniqueness Theorem)]
Assuming that there is a solution $\phi(x,y,z)$ for a given set of conductors with potentials $\phi_{0i}$, this solution must be unique.

\textbf{Proof idea:} Assume there is another solution $\psi(x,y,z)$. Since $\laplacian\phi = 0$ is linear, $\phi + c\psi$ is also a solution for $c_1\phi + c_2\psi$. In particular, let $W(x,y,z) \defeq \phi - \psi$. But then $W$ is not a solution because $\phi(\text{boundary}) = \psi(\text{boundary}) \neq 0$, but $W(\text{boundary}) = \phi(\text{boundary}) - \psi(\text{boundary}) = 0$. So $W$ is the solution to a different problem where the same conductors but all conductors held at 0 potential.

$\Rightarrow$ $W$ must be 0 everywhere (if not, then it violates the fact that it has a maximum $\phi^\text{avg}_\text{inner}$, since it has no maximum).

$\Rightarrow$ $\psi = \phi$ everywhere.

This theorem also works when there is charge: $\laplacian\phi = -\dfrac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}$.
\end{theorem}

This is powerful: if you can guess a solution (e.g.\ by image charges) that satisfies all boundary conditions, it must be \emph{the} solution.

\begin{theorem}[Corollary 3.2]
In the space inside a hollow conductor of any shape, if that space is empty of charge, the electric field is 0.

\textbf{Proof:} Since $\phi$ is equipotential on the boundaries of the conductor, and thus by the \emph{average-value condition} must be $\phi_0$ throughout the entire region enclosed by the conductor. $\Rightarrow \vect{E} = 0$.

This is related to the proof that $\vect{E} = 0$ on the inside of a conductor; since neutral atom $=$ empty space.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Why There Are No Field Lines Inside a Conductor}

If a field line must intersect 2 separate points within the conductor, but this implies a potential difference between $A$ and $B$:
\[
    \implies \text{contradicts } \vect{E} = 0
\]
This is the basis of \textbf{electrical shielding}.

\section{Image Charge Technique (Method)}

The method exploits uniqueness --- we replace the conductor with image charges chosen so the boundary condition ($\phi=0$ on the grounded plane) is satisfied, then the field in the physical region is uniquely determined.

Consider a point charge $+q$ above an infinite conducting plane; the potential is 0 at the plane.

The goal is to find the induced charge distribution on the conductor. We place a mirror image charge $-q$ at $-h$ below the plane; this two-charge system has $\phi=0$ everywhere on the plane and $\vect{E}\perp$ to the surface, satisfying the boundary conditions exactly. By uniqueness, the field above the plane is then given by Coulomb's law applied to the two-charge system.

The field perpendicular to the plane (at distance $r$ from the foot of the charge):
\[
    E_z = \frac{-2Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0(r^2+h^2)}\cos\theta = \frac{-Qh}{2\pi\epsilon_0(r^2+h^2)^{3/2}}
\]

Going back to the original setup, by Gauss's law, $E_z$ just above the plane is $\dfrac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0}$ ($\vect{E} = 0$ on the other side of the box). Thus,
\[
    \sigma(r) = \frac{-Qh}{2\pi(r^2+h^2)^{3/2}}
\]

But the total charge is $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty \sigma(r)\,dr = -Q$, \textit{not} $q$, because the remaining positive charge spreads itself on both sides of the conductor.

\subsection{General Strategy for the Image Charge Method}

Given the solution for any electrostatic problem with equipotentials located, we can get solutions for any other system made from the first by copper-plating one or more equipotential surfaces (because the equipotential lines are normal to that surface).

\section{Capacitance}

An isolated conductor carrying charge $Q$ acquires a potential $\phi_0$ (with zero at infinity), and $Q\propto\phi_0$ with a proportionality constant that depends only on the geometry.

\begin{formula}[Capacitance (isolated conductor)]
\[
    Q = C\phi_0
\]
\[
    [C] = \frac{\text{Coulomb}}{\text{Volt}} = 1\,\text{Farad}
\]
($\epsilon_0$ can be expressed as Farads/metre.)
\end{formula}

For an isolated sphere of radius $a$: $\phi_0 = Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0 a)$, so $C = 4\pi\epsilon_0 a$. For an isolated conducting disk of radius $a$: $C = 8\epsilon_0 a$, meaning a disk needs less charge to reach a given potential than a sphere of the same radius.

\subsection{Capacitance for Multiple Conductors}

Capacitance is now defined as:
\[
    C = \frac{Q}{\text{potential difference between conductors}} = \frac{Q}{\phi_a - \phi_b}
\]

A \textbf{capacitor} consists of two or more conductors separated by insulation, storing energy in the electric field between them.

\begin{example}[Parallel-Plate Capacitor]
Two flat conducting plates at separation $s$, each of area $A$.

\[
    C = \frac{Q}{\phi_1 - \phi_2} = \frac{\epsilon_0 A}{s}
\]
We assume the two plates carry equal and opposite charges $+Q$ and $-Q$, so all field lines from one plate terminate on the other.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Ex.~2: Nested Conductors]
Consider two concentric conductors. By Gauss's law, $Q_\text{outer}^\text{inner} = -Q_1$, so $Q_1$ determines the electric field between the two conductors as:
\[
    C = \frac{Q_1}{\phi_1 - \phi_2}
\]
\emph{Why does it not affect $Q_2$?} Because $Q_2^\text{outer}$ is irrelevant because (by superposition) a charge ring on the outer conductor doesn't produce any electric field inside.

\textit{Ex: Capacitance of two spherical shells --- see page 146.}
\end{example}

\section{Potentials and Charges on Several Conductors}

By combining the uniqueness theorem with superposition, the potentials and charges on a system of conductors are not independent. For three conductors enclosed by a conducting shell, specifying $\phi_1, \phi_2, \phi_3$ uniquely determines $Q_1, Q_2, Q_3$ throughout; the inner surface of the outer shell always carries $-(Q_1+Q_2+Q_3)$ by Gauss's law. The relationship is linear: specifying the potential differences (not absolute potentials) determines all the charges.

\section{Energy Stored in a Capacitor}
%%─────────────────────────────────────────────

Consider a capacitor with capacitance $C$, potential difference $\Delta\phi$, and charges $+Q$, $-Q$ on its plates. The energy stored equals the work done assembling the charge, obtained by integrating $dU = V\,dq$ as charge is transferred:
\[
  W = \frac{1}{C}\int_0^{Q_f} Q\,dQ = \frac{Q_f^2}{2C} \;\defeq\; U_C
  \qquad \text{-- Energy stored in a Capacitor}
\]

The energy can be written three equivalent ways:

\begin{formula}[Energy stored in a capacitor]
\[
  U_C = \frac{Q^2}{2C} = \frac{Q\Phi}{2} = \frac{1}{2}C\Phi^2
\]
\end{formula}

\noindent For a parallel-plate capacitor with area $A$ and separation $s$:

\[
  C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{s}
  \implies
  U_C = \frac{1}{2}\!\left(\frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{s}\right)\!(E\,s)^2
       = \frac{\varepsilon_0 E^2}{2}\cdot \text{Volume}
\]
where $E = \Phi/s$.

\medskip
\noindent This equation also applies to an \emph{isolated charged conductor}. For an isolated sphere:
\[
  C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 a, \qquad
  U_C = \frac{1}{2}Cq^2 \;=\; \frac{1}{2}(4\pi\varepsilon_0 a)\Phi^2
      = \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 a}
\]
which agrees with the earlier calculation for energy stored in the electric field of a charged sphere.

\section{Force Required to Keep Capacitor Plates Apart}
%%─────────────────────────────────────────────

For a general capacitor whose capacitance $C(x)$ depends on plate separation $x$, the plates attract each other and an external force $F_\text{ext}$ is needed to hold them apart. Increasing $x$ by $\Delta x$ at constant $Q$ (one plate fixed) requires the external agent to do work equal to the change in stored energy:

\begin{align*}
  F_\text{ext}\,\Delta x &= \Delta U = \frac{Q^2}{2}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\!\left(\frac{1}{C}\right)\Delta x \\[4pt]
  \implies\quad F_\text{ext} &= \frac{Q^2}{2}\,\frac{d}{dx}\!\left(\frac{1}{C}\right)
\end{align*}

\section{Conductors and Current Density (Introduction)}
%%─────────────────────────────────────────────

In a conductor carrying current, \textbf{conductivity} $\sigma$ (distinct from surface charge density) relates current density to field via Ohm's law:

\begin{formula}[Ohm's Law (microscopic)]
\[
  \vect{J} = \sigma\,\vect{E}
\]
\end{formula}

\noindent where $\vect{J}$ is current density (Amps/m$^2$) and $[\sigma] = \tfrac{\text{Amp}}{\text{m}^2}\big/\tfrac{\text{V}}{\text{m}}$.

\[
  I = \vect{J}\cdot\hat{A} = S\,\vect{E}\cdot\hat{A}
  \qquad \text{(Amps = Coulombs/sec)}
\]
\[
  I = \int \vect{J}\cdot d\vect{A}
\]

Charge carriers scatter off lattice defects and acquire a finite drift velocity rather than accelerating freely:

\[
  I = \vect{J}\cdot A = S\,\vect{E}\cdot\hat{A} = \left(\frac{\sigma A}{s}\right)V
\]

\noindent From $I = GV$, $V = \tfrac{1}{G} \cdot I$, so $G = \tfrac{\sigma A}{s}$;
resistance $R = \tfrac{1}{G} = \tfrac{s}{\sigma A}$.
\[
  I = \frac{V}{R}, \qquad [R] \;=\; \left(\frac{6.37}{\text{Amp}}\right)\cdot\frac{1\;\text{Volt}}{1\;\text{Amp}}
\]
\[
  \rho = \frac{1}{\sigma} = \text{resistivity}; \quad R = \frac{\rho\,\ell}{A}
\]

\subsubsection*{Case (i): Charge above a Grounded Plane}

Setup: charge $Q$ at $(0,0,d)$, image charge $-Q$ at $(0,0,-d)$, observation point $\vec{r}=(x,y,z)$, surface at $z=0$.

\[
  \vect{E} = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{x\,\hat{x} + y\,\hat{y} + (z-d)\,\hat{z}}{\bigl(x^2+y^2+(z-d)^2\bigr)^{3/2}}
  + \frac{-Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{x\,\hat{x} + y\,\hat{y} + (z+d)\,\hat{z}}{\bigl(x^2+y^2+(z+d)^2\bigr)^{3/2}}
\]

\noindent At $z=0$:
\[
  \vect{E}(x,y,0) = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{-2d\,\hat{z}}{(x^2+y^2+d^2)^{3/2}}
                  = \frac{-Qd\,\hat{z}}{2\pi\varepsilon_0(x^2+y^2+d^2)^{3/2}}
\]

\noindent In cylindrical coordinates ($r,\theta,\phi$):
\[
  E(r,\theta) = \frac{-Qd\,\hat{z}}{2\pi\varepsilon_0(r^2+d^2)^{3/2}}
\]

\noindent From the boundary condition $E = \sigma/\varepsilon_0$:
\[
  \sigma = \varepsilon_0\,\vect{E}\cdot\hat{z} = \frac{-Qd}{2\pi(r^2+d^2)^{3/2}}
\]

\noindent To find total charge:
\[
  \int_0^\infty \sigma\cdot 2\pi r\,dr = \int_0^\infty \frac{-Qd}{2\pi(r^2+d^2)}\cdot 2\pi r\,dr
  + Qd\cdot\frac{1}{(r^2-d^2)^{1/2}}\bigg|
\]
(The total induced charge is $-Q$; it is repelled to $-z$ infinity.)

\section{Capacitance: Further Examples}
%%─────────────────────────────────────────────

\begin{definition}[Capacitor]
A capacitor consists of two conductors with potentials $\phi_1$ and $\phi_2$, charges $+q$ and $-q$:
\[
  Q = C\bigl(|\phi_1 - \phi_2|\bigr) \quad \text{only valid if you move } Q
  \text{ from one previously neutral conductor}
\]
\[
  Q \approx C\Phi = C|\Delta\phi|
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Coaxial Cylindrical Capacitor]
Inner radius $a$ (charge $+Q$), outer radius $b$ (charge $-Q$), length $L\to\infty$;
linear charge density $\lambda = Q/L$.

By Gauss's law:
\[
  |\vect{E}|\cdot 2\pi r L = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0}
  \implies |\vect{E}| = \frac{Q}{2\pi r L\varepsilon_0} = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi r\varepsilon_0}
\]
\[
  |\Delta\phi| = \int_a^b \vect{E}\,dr = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\!\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)
\]
Capacitance per unit length:
\[
  \frac{C}{L} \equiv \frac{\text{capacitance}}{\text{unit length}} = \frac{Q}{L\,|\Delta\phi|}
            = \frac{\lambda\cdot 2\pi\varepsilon_0}{\lambda\ln(b/a)} = \frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0}{\ln(b/a)}
\]
\end{example}

\subsection{Capacitor Paradox}
%%─────────────────────────────────────────────

The paradox is whether connecting two capacitors in this configuration leaves the field unchanged (A) or causes charge to redistribute so that all field is confined between the inner pair of plates (B). The answer is B: option A contains a logical flaw because $\Delta\phi$ is defined relative to infinity, and assuming nothing changes ignores the fact that the connected plates must equilibrate to a common potential consistent with the boundary conditions at infinity.

\medskip\noindent Note: equilibrium state.
