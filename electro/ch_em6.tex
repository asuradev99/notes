\chapter{Current}

\section{Electric Current}

Electric current is charge in motion; 1 ampere equals 1 coulomb per second. The key microscopic quantities are the charge carrier density $n$, drift velocity $\vect{u}$, charge $q$, and cross-sectional area vector $\vect{a}$.

\end{figure}

The volume swept through the frame in time $\Delta t$ is $\vect{a}\cdot\vect{u}\,\Delta t$, containing on average $n(\vect{a}\cdot\vect{u}\,\Delta t)$ particles, giving a current:

\begin{formula}[Current through a frame]
\[
    I_a = \frac{q\!\left(n\,\vect{a}\cdot\vect{u}\,\Delta t\right)}{\Delta t} = nq\,\vect{a}\cdot\vect{u}
\]
\end{formula}

If there are multiple kinds of particles moving in the same direction:
\[
    I_a = n_1 q_1\,\vect{a}\cdot\vect{u}_1 + n_2 q_2\,\vect{a}\cdot\vect{u}_2 + \cdots
    = \vect{a}\cdot\sum_k n_k q_k \vect{u}_k
\]

\begin{definition}[Current Density]
\[
    \vect{J} \defeq \sum_k n_k q_k \vect{u}_k
\]
with units $\text{Amperes}/\text{m}^2$, so that $I_a = \vect{a}\cdot\vect{J}$.
\end{definition}

In a typical conductor, electrons move in every direction; the mean velocity averaged over all electrons is:

\begin{formula}[Mean electron velocity]
\[
    \bar{\vect{u}} = \frac{1}{N_e}\sum_k n_k \vect{u}_k
\]
where $N_e$ is the number of electrons per unit volume (total).
\end{formula}

Combining this with the definition of $\vect{J}$:

\begin{formula}[Electron current density]
\[
    \vect{J}_e = -eN_e\bar{\vect{u}}_e
\]
Units: $\dfrac{\text{charge}}{\text{area}}\cdot\dfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$.
\end{formula}

\section{Steady Currents and Charge Conservation}

The total current through a surface is the flux of $\vect{J}$:

\begin{formula}[Total current through a surface]
\[
    I = \int_S \vect{J}\cdot d\vect{a}
\]
\end{formula}

In steady state, no charge accumulates anywhere, so the net flux of $\vect{J}$ through any closed surface is zero. By the divergence theorem, this gives $\div\vect{J} = 0$ everywhere. More generally, charge conservation requires the continuity equation $\div\vect{J} = -\partial\rho/\partial t$.

\begin{formula}[Divergence-free condition for steady currents]
\[
    \div\vect{J} = 0 \quad\text{at every point in space}
\]
\end{formula}

In steady-state: $\oint \vect{J}\cdot d\vect{a} = 0$.

By Gauss's theorem:
\[
    \int \nabla\cdot\vect{J}\,dV = \oint \vect{J}\cdot d\vect{a}
    \quad\Rightarrow\quad \nabla\cdot\vect{J} = 0
\]

More generally, if $J$ is \emph{not} steady:
\[
    \int_S \vect{J}\cdot d\vect{a} = -\dv{}{t}\int_V \rho\,dV = -\dv{Q}{t}
\]

By Gauss's theorem:

\begin{formula}[Continuity Equation]
\[
    \div\vect{J} = -\pdv{\rho}{t}
\]
\end{formula}

Note: $\rho = \dot\rho \neq 0 \Rightarrow I \neq 0$.

\section{Ohm's Law}

It is empirically common that:

\begin{formula}[Ohm's Law (microscopic)]
\[
    \vect{J} = \sigma\vect{E}
    \quad\text{where } \sigma = \text{conductivity},\quad [\sigma] = \frac{[J]}{[E]} = \Omega^{-1}\text{m}^{-1}
\]
\end{formula}

Microscopically, $\vect{J} = \sigma\vect{E}$ arises because carriers accelerated by $\vect{E}$ scatter off the lattice, reaching a steady drift velocity proportional to the field. The conductivity $\sigma$ depends on carrier density and mean collision time, and varies by many orders of magnitude across materials --- from metals ($\sigma \sim 10^7\,\Omega^{-1}\text{m}^{-1}$) to insulators ($\sigma \sim 10^{-15}\,\Omega^{-1}\text{m}^{-1}$).

Unlike in electrostatics, the field inside a current-carrying conductor is \emph{not} zero. Charges are driven by the external EMF and reach a steady drift, not accelerated indefinitely, because they scatter off the lattice. The field is maintained by surface charges that build up on the conductor surface.

In isotropic materials the conductivity is the same in all directions, so $I \propto J$ and $V \propto E$; since $\vect{J}\propto\vect{E}$, we get $I \propto V$:

\[
    V = IR \quad\text{--- resistance of the conductor; depends on size and shape}
\]

\textbf{Deriving resistance} (for a bar of cross-section $A$ and length $L$):

\begin{formula}[Resistance of a bar]
\[
    R = \frac{V}{I} = \frac{EL}{AJ} = \frac{L}{A\sigma}
\]
\end{formula}

This assumes the current density is uniform over the cross-section, $J$ is uniform near the edges, and the bar is surrounded by a non-conducting medium. If $\sigma$ is constant, $\div\vect{J}=0$ implies $\div\vect{E}=0$, so there is no bulk charge density. If $\sigma$ is not constant, surface charge builds up at the boundary between conductivities.

\begin{definition}[Resistivity]
Resistivity is the reciprocal of conductivity:
\[
    \rho = \frac{1}{\sigma}
\]
Thus:
\[
    R = \frac{\rho L}{A},
    \quad [R] = \text{Ohm} = \frac{1\,\text{volt}}{1\,\text{ampere}},
    \quad [\rho] = \text{Ohm}\cdot\text{meter}
\]
\end{definition}

When dealing with current in wires, we generally assume the wire is neutral, but in fact surface charges must be present to maintain the field that drives the current.
