\chapter{Special Relativity}

Special relativity is not a detour: it is the key that unlocks magnetism. Purcell's central insight is that magnetism is a relativistic consequence of Coulomb's law — what one observer sees as a purely electric force, another (moving) observer sees as a combination of electric and magnetic forces. This approach reveals that $\vect{E}$ and $\vect{B}$ are not independent entities but two aspects of a single electromagnetic field.

\section{Einstein's Postulates}

There are two: in every inertial reference frame the laws of physics are the same, and in all reference frames the speed of light is constant.

These two postulates, together with the principle that space is homogeneous and isotropic, uniquely determine the Lorentz transformation.

\subsection*{Time Dilation}

Consider a light clock on a train moving with speed $v$.

On the train, the light travels straight up and back a distance $h$, taking
\[
  \Delta t_{\text{train}} = \frac{h}{c}.
\]
Seen from the ground, the light traces a longer diagonal path of length $d_{\text{total}} = \sqrt{h^2 + (v\,\Delta t_g)^2}$, so
\[
  \Delta t_g = \frac{\sqrt{h^2 + (v\,\Delta t_g)^2}}{c} > \Delta t_{\text{train}}.
\]
Setting the light-travel time equal in both frames gives
\[
  \Delta t = \frac{\Delta t'}{\sqrt{1 - \dfrac{v^2}{c^2}}} = \gamma\,\Delta t'
\]
where $\Delta t' = h/c$ (train clock) and $\Delta t = \tfrac{1}{c}\sqrt{h^2+(v\Delta t)^2}$ (ground clock).

\subsection*{Clock Synchronization}

Two clocks separated by distance $x$ are synchronized by flashing light from the midpoint ($t=0$). When the light reaches the clocks, the times are set forward by $x/c$.

Suppose all clocks are synchronized. Stationary observers note readings on train clocks shift across.

In the train's reference frame:
\[
  t' = -\gamma\,\frac{v}{c^2}\,x
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  t = \gamma\,\frac{v}{c^2}\,x'
\]

\subsection*{Length Contraction (Train)}

Consider a train of rest-length $\Delta x'$ moving with speed $v$.

Time for a light beam to go to the end and back (in the ground frame):
\[
  \Delta t = \frac{2\Delta x}{c}
\]
In the train's frame:
\[
  \Delta t' = \frac{2\Delta x'}{c} = \Delta t\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}} = \frac{\Delta t}{\gamma}
\]

Working out the two legs separately:
\begin{align*}
  \Delta t_1 &= \frac{\Delta x + v\,\Delta t_1}{c} = \frac{\Delta x}{c - v} \\
  \Delta t_2 &= \frac{\Delta x - v\,\Delta t_2}{c} = \frac{\Delta x}{c + v}
\end{align*}
\[
  \text{thus}\quad \Delta t_{\text{total}} = \frac{2\Delta x/c}{1 - v^2/c^2}
\]

From this:
\[
  \Delta t' = \frac{1}{\gamma}\,\Delta t
  \qquad
  \left(\text{why?}\quad \Delta x = \tfrac{v}{2}\,\Delta t\,\tfrac{1}{\gamma^2}\right)
\]
\[
  \Rightarrow\quad \Delta x = \Delta x'\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}
\]

\chapter{Relativity and Electromagnetism}

\section{Magnetic Forces}

The entire force on a particle carrying a charge $q$ is given by
\begin{formula}[Lorentz Force]
\[
  \vect{F} = q\vect{E} + q\vect{v}\times\vect{B} \quad \leftarrow \text{magnetic force}
\]
\end{formula}
where the first term is the electric force and the second is the magnetic force.

\textbf{How do we find $\vect{B}$?} First determine $\vect{E}$ from the force on a stationary test charge (so $v=0$). Then measure the additional force on a moving charge for at least two independent velocity directions; the unique $\vect{B}$ that accounts for both additional forces is the magnetic field.

\section{Measurement of Charge in Motion}

The electric charge of a moving particle is not obviously well-defined, since we ordinarily extract charge from the force on a stationary test particle. The natural extension is to define $Q$ using Gauss's law: place a closed sensor surface $S$ around the region and measure the total flux when $Q$ passes through.

\subsection*{Charge in a Moving Frame}

\[
  Q = \epsilon_0 \int_{S(t)} \vect{E}\cdot d\vect{a}
\]

Here $S$ is fixed in frame $F$ while $Q$ moves through it. Gauss's law remains valid even when the charge is moving, which is the content of charge invariance.

\section*{Invariance of Charge}

Charge invariance means the total charge in a system is the same number in all inertial frames — not just conserved in time, but frame-independent. This is stronger than conservation; mass, by contrast, is not invariant in the same way. Crucially, it is charge invariance (not merely conservation) that allows Gauss's law to hold in all frames and guarantees that the field transformations are consistent:
\[
  \int_{S(t)} \vect{E}\cdot d\vect{a} = \int_{S'(t')} \vect{E}'\cdot d\vect{a}'
\]
(measured at different times due to relativistic time dilation). The experimental support for charge invariance is extensive and precise.

\section{Electric Field in Different Frames}

If charge is invariant under Lorentz transformations, the electric field $\vect{E}$ must transform in a specific way. The question is: if an observer in frame $F$ measures $\vect{E}$ at $(x,y)$, what field is measured at the same spacetime point in $F'$? We answer this by considering two stationary sheets of charge $\sigma$ and $-\sigma$.

In frame $F'$ moving to the left with speed $v$, the sheets undergo length contraction: the separation contracts to $b' = \gamma b$. Since total charge is invariant, the surface charge density must change by the inverse factor, $\sigma' = \sigma/\gamma = \sqrt{1-\beta^2}\,\sigma$ (so that $\sigma' A' = \sigma A$). Applying Gauss's law to a stationary box in $F'$ gives
\[
  E_2' = \frac{\sigma'}{\epsilon_0} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{1-\beta^2}\,\epsilon_0} = \gamma\,\frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0} = \gamma E_2.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Transformation of Electric Field]
The component of $\vect{E}$ parallel to the relative velocity is unchanged, while the transverse component is enhanced by $\gamma$:
\begin{align*}
  E'_{\parallel} &= E_{\parallel} \\
  E'_{\perp} &= \gamma E_{\perp}
\end{align*}
\end{theorem}

This asymmetry is the seed of the magnetic field: a purely electric field in one frame has a transverse magnetic component in another. The transverse component is smallest in the rest frame of the sources and grows in any frame where the sources move.

\subsection*{Example: Tilted Sheet ($v = 0.6c$)}

Find the surface charge density $\sigma'$ and the electric field. For $v = 0.6c$ we have $\gamma = 4/3$. The parallel length of the sheet is contracted while the perpendicular extent is unchanged, so $\sigma' = (1.1043)\,\sigma$ by charge conservation. The field in $F$ is $\vect{E}_F = \sigma/(2\epsilon_0)$ perpendicular to the sheet; decomposing into parallel and transverse components relative to the boost direction and applying the transformation rules,
\[
  E'_{\parallel} = E_{\parallel} = \frac{E}{\sqrt{2}},
  \qquad
  E'_{\perp} = \gamma E_{\perp} = \gamma\,\frac{E}{\sqrt{2}}.
\]
The magnitude of the transformed field is:
\[
  |E'| = \sqrt{E_\parallel'^2 + E_\perp'^2} = \frac{E}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{5}{4}\right)^2} = 1.1319\,E.
\]

\section{Field of a Moving Point Charge}

Suppose there is a point charge $Q$ at rest at the origin in reference frame $F$. In $F$:
\begin{align*}
  E_x &= \frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2}\cos\theta = \frac{Qx}{4\pi\epsilon_0(x^2+z^2)^{3/2}} \\
  E_z &= \frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2}\sin\theta = \frac{Qz}{4\pi\epsilon_0(y^2+z^2)^{3/2}}
\end{align*}
Let event $A$ (when $F$ and $F'$ coincide at the origin) be $(0,0,0,0)$ and event $B$ be $(x,y,z,t)$. Expressing $x$, $z$ in terms of primed coordinates via the Lorentz transformation ($x = \gamma x' - \gamma\beta ct'$, $y = y'$, $z = z'$) and applying the field transformation rules $E'_x = E_x$, $E'_z = \gamma E_z$ at $t'=0$:
\begin{align*}
  E'_x = E_x &= \frac{Q(\gamma x')}{4\pi\epsilon_0\left[(\gamma x')^2 + z'^2\right]^{3/2}} \\
  E'_z = \gamma E_z &= \frac{\gamma(Qz')}{4\pi\epsilon_0\left[(\gamma x')^2 + z'^2\right]^{3/2}}
\end{align*}
Since $E'_z/E'_x = z'/x'$, the field $\vect{E}'$ points radially outward from the instantaneous position of $Q$. Combining components gives:

\begin{formula}[Field of a Moving Point Charge]
\[
  E' = \frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r'^2}\left(\frac{1-\beta^2}{(1-\beta^2\sin^2\theta')^{3/2}}\right)
\]
where $\theta'$ is the angle between the radius vector and the velocity direction.
\end{formula}

\section{Force on a Moving Charge}

Consider a test charge moving with velocity $\vect{v}$ in a field produced by sources at rest in the lab frame $F$. The test charge has its own rest frame $F'$. The momentum components transform as
\[
  \frac{dp_{\parallel}}{dt} = \frac{dp'_{\parallel}}{dt}, \qquad
  \frac{dp_{\perp}}{dt} = \frac{1}{\gamma}\frac{dp'_{\perp}}{dt}
  \quad \text{(for a particle at rest in } F').
\]
The transverse force is larger in the particle's frame $F'$ because in $F'$ the sources are moving and appear Lorentz-contracted, enhancing the transverse field: $E'_\perp = \gamma E_\perp$, $E'_\parallel = E_\parallel$. The net effect is that the transverse force seen in the lab is $F_\perp = F'_\perp/\gamma$, which is smaller — the field enhancement and the momentum transformation factor of $1/\gamma$ work in opposite directions.

\section{Interaction Between Moving Charges}

Why do wires going in opposite directions repel?
\[
  I = -\lambda_0 v_0
\]

Two key facts govern this: (1) the transverse electric field due to a charge is smallest in the frame of that charge, and (2) the transverse force on a particle is largest in the frame of that particle. Together, they determine how forces transform between frames.

This is the origin of the magnetic force between parallel wires. In the rest frame of one set of charge carriers, there is a net electric field that attracts or repels the wire. Boosting back to the lab frame, this becomes the familiar magnetic force $\vect{F} = I\vect{\ell}\times\vect{B}$.

\section*{Chapter Summary}

Charged particles moving in a magnetic field experience a force $\vect{F} = q\vect{v}\times\vect{B}$. Charge is invariant, so Gauss's law works in all frames. Take a collection of charges moving with respect to the lab frame, acting as the source of an electric field; the components of that field transform as
\[
  E_{\parallel}^{\text{lab}} = E_{\parallel}^{\text{source}}
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  E_{\perp}^{\text{lab}} = \gamma E_{\perp}^{\text{source}}
\]
and in particular the field of a point charge moving with constant velocity $v = \beta c$ is radial with magnitude
\[
  E = \frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2}\frac{1-\beta^2}{(1-\beta^2\sin^2\theta)^{3/2}}
\]
The force on a charge $q$ moving through the electric field $\vect{E}$ arising from stationary charges is simply $q\vect{E}$; but if that charge moves with respect to other charges that are themselves moving in the lab frame (e.g.\ in a wire), it feels a magnetic force as well.

The magnetic field is not a separate entity introduced ad hoc — it is the relativistic transform of the electric field, required by the invariance of charge.

A \textbf{frame of reference} is a coordinate system equipped with measuring rods and stationary synchronized clocks; space is assumed \underline{homogeneous} and \underline{isotropic}. An \textbf{event} is something that happens at a definite location and time, identified by coordinates $(x, y, z, t)$. An \textbf{inertial frame} is one in which Newton's first law holds. Special relativity asserts that no inertial frame is privileged over any other.

\section{Lorentz Transformations}

Consider two events $A$ and $B$ observed in an inertial frame $F$. The spacetime displacement is $\Delta x_F = (x_B - x_A,\; y_B - y_A,\; z_B - z_A,\; t_B - t_A)$. In frame $F'$ moving with speed $v$ in the $\hat{x}$ direction, the same displacement is related by:

\begin{formula}[Lorentz Transformation]
\begin{align*}
  (1)&\quad x'_B - x'_A = \gamma(x_B - x_A) - \beta\gamma c(t_B - t_A) \\
  (2)&\quad y'_B - y'_A = y_B - y_A \\
  (3)&\quad z'_B - z'_A = z_B - z_A \\
  (4)&\quad t'_B - t'_A = \gamma(t_B - t_A) - \beta\gamma(x_B - x_A)/c
\end{align*}
where $c$ is the speed of light, $\beta = \dfrac{v}{c}$, $\gamma = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-\beta^2}}$.
\end{formula}

The inverse transformation is identical with $v \to -v$ (swap primed and unprimed). Two events that are simultaneous in $F$ ($t_B - t_A = 0$) are generally not simultaneous in $F'$ unless they also share the same $x$-coordinate ($x_B - x_A = 0$).

\begin{example}[Length Contraction via Lorentz]
Consider a rod in $F'$ parallel to the $x'$ axis, going from $x'_A$ to $x'_B$, so its rest length is $x'_B - x'_A$. In frame $F$, the length is defined as the distance between two points that the rod's ends pass through simultaneously ($t_B - t_A = 0$), giving:
\[
  x_B - x_A = \frac{1}{\gamma}(x'_B - x'_A).
\]
The moving rod is thus shorter in $F$ by a factor of $1/\gamma$ — this is \textbf{Lorentz contraction}.
\end{example}

Now consider one of the clocks in $F'$. Suppose it records itself passing two clocks in $F$ at times $t'_A$ and $t'_B$, while those clocks record $t_A$ and $t_B$. Thus, since in $F'$ we know that $x_B - x_A = v(t_B - t_A)$:
\begin{align*}
  t'_B - t'_A &= \gamma(t_B - t_A) - \beta\gamma v(t_B - t_A) \\
               &= \gamma(t_B - t_A)(1 - \beta^2)
               = \boxed{\frac{1}{\gamma}(t_B - t_A)}
               \quad\to\text{ called time dilation}
\end{align*}
(don't try to move fast!)

In summary: \underline{moving clocks run slowly by a factor of $\dfrac{1}{\gamma}$} ($0 < \tfrac{1}{\gamma} < 1$, in the stationary frame $F$) and moving objects are shortened by $\dfrac{1}{\gamma}$.

\section{Velocity Addition}

Suppose an object is moving in the positive $x$ direction in frame $F$ with velocity $u_x$; what is its velocity in frame $F'$?

Setting $x = 0$ at $t = 0$, the object's coordinates in $F$ are $x = u_x t$. Substituting into the Lorentz transformation equations (1) and (4):
\begin{align*}
  x' &= \gamma u_x t - \beta\gamma ct, \qquad t' = \gamma t - \beta\gamma\frac{u_x t}{c}.
\end{align*}
Dividing:
\[
  u'_x = \frac{x'}{t'} = \frac{u_x - v}{1 - \dfrac{u_x v}{c^2}}.
\]

\begin{formula}[Velocity Transformation Formula]
\[
  u'_x = \frac{u_x - v}{1 - \dfrac{u_x v}{c^2}}
\]
\end{formula}

The inverse transformation is not simply $v \to -v$ for the velocity (since $u_x \neq -u'_x$); instead:
\[
  u_x = \frac{u'_x + v}{1 - \dfrac{u'_x v}{c^2}}.
\]
If there is also a perpendicular velocity component $u_y$, it transforms differently: since $y' = y = u_y t$ and $t' = \gamma t(1 - \beta u_x/c)$,
\[
  u'_y = \frac{u_y}{\gamma(1 - u_x v/c^2)}.
\]
The perpendicular velocity is reduced by a factor of $\gamma$ compared with the parallel formula.

\section{Relativistic Energy and Momentum}

If a particle is moving with velocity $\vect{u}$ in an inertial frame $F_1$:

\begin{formula}[Relativistic Momentum and Energy]
\[
  \vect{p} = \gamma m_0 \vect{u}
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  E = \gamma m_0 c^2
\]
where $m_0$ is the \underline{rest mass} and $\gamma = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1 - u^2/c^2}}$ (where $\vect{u}$ is same as above).

This $E$ is relativistic energy; \emph{not} kinetic or potential energy.
\end{formula}

\subsection*{Momentum Transformation}

($\beta = v/c$) gives the momentum and energy of a particle observed in a frame $F'$, given the information in $F$:
\begin{align*}
  p'_x &= \gamma p_x - \beta\gamma\frac{E}{c} \\
  p'_y &= p_y \\
  p'_z &= p_z \\
  E'   &= \gamma E - \beta\gamma c p_x
\end{align*}
These form a \textit{four-vector}.

Force is $F = d\vect{p}/dt$ where $\vect{p}$ and $t$ are both measured in the same frame. Consider a particle of rest mass $m_0$ initially at rest in frame $F$; a force $f_x$ acts for a short time $\Delta t$. In that interval $p_x$ grows from $0$ to $f_x\,\Delta t$, the displacement is $\Delta x \approx \tfrac{1}{2}(f_x/m_0)(\Delta t)^2$, and the kinetic energy gained is
\[
  \Delta E = \frac{(f_x \Delta t)^2}{2m_0}
  \quad\text{(valid when the speed is small enough for Newtonian mechanics).}
\]
To find $dp'/dt$ in $F'$, one uses the momentum transformation rules together with these expressions.

\chapter{The Magnetic Field}

\section*{Ampere's Law}

\begin{formula}[Ampere's Law]
\[
  \oint_{\partial S} \vect{B}\cdot d\vect{\ell} = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}}
\]
\end{formula}

\textbf{Important:} direction defined by right-hand rule.

\section*{Symmetry}

By symmetry for a long straight wire:
\[
  \oint \vect{B}\cdot ds = |\vect{B}|\cdot 2\pi r = \mu_0 I
  \quad\Rightarrow\quad
  |\vect{B}| = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}\,\hat{\phi}
\]

First, solve the $\vect{B}$ field of a solid cylindrical wire:

\begin{align*}
  \oint \vect{B}\cdot d\vect{\ell} &= \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}} \\
  |\vect{B}|\cdot 2\pi r &= \mu_0 J \pi r^2 \\
  |\vect{B}| &= \frac{\mu_0 J r}{2}\,\hat{\phi} \quad\text{inside} \quad(\phi \text{ direction}) \\
             &= \frac{\mu_0 J r}{2}(\hat{z}\times\hat{r}) \\
             &= \frac{\mu_0}{2}\vect{J}\times\vect{r}
\end{align*}

\section{Definition of the Magnetic Field}

\begin{definition}[Magnetic Field]
\[
  \vect{F} = q\vect{E} + q\vect{v}\times\vect{B}
\]
$\vect{B}$ is the magnetic field at that same time and place; $\vect{E}$ is the electric field at the moving particle's position in the \underline{stationary frame}.
\end{definition}

\subsection*{Magnetic Field of a Straight Infinite Wire}

\[
  \vect{B} = \hat{z}\,\frac{1}{2\pi\epsilon_0 c^2} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}\,\hat{\phi}
  \qquad\text{($B$ is in units of Teslas)}
\]
\begin{formula}[Relation between $\mu_0$ and $\epsilon_0$]
\[
  \frac{1}{\epsilon_0 c^2} = \mu_0 = 4\pi\times 10^{-7}\,\frac{\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}}{\text{C}^2}
\]
\end{formula}

\subsection*{Example: Calculating Magnetic Field Between Two Wires}

\begin{align*}
  B_1 &= \frac{\mu_0 I_1}{2\pi r} \\
  I_2 &= n_2\, q_2\, v_2
\end{align*}

By $F = qE + q(v\times B)$: since $v\perp B_1$ and $qE=0$:
\[
  F_{q_2} = q_2 v_2 B_1
\]
Thus the force on each meter length of wire 2 is $n_2\,q_2\,v_2\,B_1 = I_2 B_1$.

Thus the force on a length $\ell$ of wire 2 is:
\[
  F = I_2 B_1 \ell = \frac{\mu_0 I_1 I_2 \ell}{2\pi r}
\]

\subsection*{Force on a Small Current-Carrying Wire}

For a small element of wire of length $d\ell$ carrying current $I = \lambda v$, the charge in that element is $dq = \lambda\,d\ell$. The magnetic force is $dF = dq\,\vect{v}\times\vect{B}$, which gives:

\begin{formula}[Force on a Current Element]
\[
    d\vect{F} = \lambda v\,d\vect{\ell}\times\vect{B} = I\,d\vect{\ell}\times\vect{B}
\]
where $d\vect{\ell}$ gives the direction and magnitude of the small piece.
\end{formula}

For two parallel wires this yields the special result $F = I_2 B_1 \ell$.

\section{Properties of the Magnetic Field}

The $\hat{z}$ direction is defined with respect to the plane created by the current and the point of interest. The line integral of $\vect{B}$ around any closed path that does not enclose current is zero.

\begin{formula}[Ampere's Law]
\[
  \oint \vect{B}\cdot d\vect{s} = \mu_0 I_c
  \qquad (I_c = \text{current enclosed by path})
\]
\end{formula}

Note the sign convention: by the right-hand rule, current going into the page (clockwise loop) is positive; current out of the page (counter-clockwise loop) is negative.

\subsection*{More General Ampere's Law}

Since $\div\vect{J}=0$ in magnetostatics and the enclosed current is $I_c = \int_S \vect{J}\cdot d\vect{a}$, the integral form $\oint_C \vect{B}\cdot d\vect{s} = \mu_0 I_c$ leads directly to the differential form:
\[
  \boxed{\curl\vect{B} = \mu_0\vect{J}} \quad (1)
\]
Together with the no-monopole condition:
\[
  \boxed{\div\vect{B} = 0} \quad (2)
\]
these two equations, given $\vect{J}$, uniquely determine $\vect{B}$ (by the Helmholtz theorem, provided $\vect{B}$ vanishes at infinity).

\begin{theorem}[Uniqueness Theorem]
Assuming $\vect{B}$ vanishes at infinity, (1) and (2) determine $\vect{B}$, if $\vect{J}$ is given --- a special case of the \underline{Helmholtz Theorem}. (Proof: see Purcell p.\ 292.)
\end{theorem}

\section*{Vector Potential}

The vector potential $\vect{A}$ represents $\vect{B}$ as $\curl\vect{A}$ (analogue of $\vect{E} = -\grad\phi$). In the Coulomb gauge $\div\vect{A}=0$, the condition $\curl(\curl\vect{A}) = \mu_0\vect{J}$ reduces to $\laplacian\vect{A} = -\mu_0\vect{J}$, whose solution is directly analogous to the electrostatic potential:
\[
  \vect{A}(x_1,y_1,z_1) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{\vect{J}(x_2,y_2,z_2)\,dV_2}{r_{12}}.
\]

\section{Field of Any Current-Carrying Wire}

For an arbitrary loop of wire:
\[
  J = \frac{I}{a}, \qquad dV_2 = a\,d\ell
  \quad\Rightarrow\quad J\,dV_2 = I\,d\ell
  \quad\Rightarrow\quad \vect{J}\,dV_2 = I\,d\vect{\ell}
\]
\[
  \text{Thus}\quad \vect{A} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\int\frac{d\vect{\ell}}{r_{12}}
\]

In the $\hat{\phi}$ direction (at a point $(x, y, 0)$):
\[
  d\vect{A} = \hat{x}\,\frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\frac{d\ell}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}}
\]
\[
  d\vect{B} = \curl(d\vect{A}) = \hat{z}\left(-\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial y}\right)
  = \hat{z}\,\frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\frac{y\,d\ell}{(x^2+y^2)^{3/2}}
  = \hat{z}\,\frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\frac{\sin\phi\,d\ell}{r^2}
\]

\begin{formula}[Biot--Savart Law]
\[
  d\vect{B} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\frac{d\vect{\ell}\times\hat{r}}{r^2}
  \qquad\text{or}\qquad
  d\vect{B} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\frac{d\vect{\ell}\times\vect{r}}{r^3}
\]
\end{formula}

The gauge freedom in $\vect{A}$ means one can add any gradient $\grad\psi$ (which gives zero when integrated around a closed path, since $\curl(\grad\psi)=0$) without changing $\vect{B}$.

\section{Fields of Rings and Coils}

At the center of the ring:
\[
  \boxed{B_z = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2b}}
\]

Solenoid: equivalent to a stack of current rings.
\[
  r = \frac{b}{2n\theta}
\]

Using the geometry: $x = r\,d\theta$, $\sin\theta = x/r$, so $h = x/\sin\theta$:
\[
  \Rightarrow\quad dI = I\cdot n\,\frac{r\,d\theta}{\sin\theta}
\]
By the ring formula:
\[
  dB_z = (2\pi)\frac{\mu_0 b^2}{2r^3} = \left(\frac{nI\,r\,d\theta}{\sin\theta}\right)\frac{\mu_0 b^2}{2r^3}
  = \frac{\mu_0 nI}{2}\sin\theta\,d\theta
\]
\[
  B_z = \frac{\mu_0 nI}{2}\int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2}\sin\theta\,d\theta
      = \frac{\mu_0 nI}{2}(\cos\theta_1 - \cos\theta_2)
\]
If $\theta_1 = 0$ and $\theta_2 = \pi$ (infinite coil):
\[
  \boxed{B_z = \mu_0 n I}
\]

\subsection*{Class Notes: Plane of Current}

Current per unit length is $I/w$. A strip of width $dx$ carries current $dI = I\,d\vec{\ell} = \dfrac{I}{w}\,dx$.

\[
  B = \int_{-w/2}^{w/2} \frac{\mu_0\,dI}{2\pi r\cdot w}\cdot\cos\theta\,dx
\]
