\section{Thermal Equilibrium and Temperature}
% ============================================================

\begin{definition}[Temperature]
Temperature is the quantity that is equal for two objects that have been in thermal contact for
sufficiently long, that is, that have reached \emph{thermal equilibrium}. Operationally, it
measures the tendency of an object to give up energy to its surroundings: a hotter object
transfers energy to a cooler one until equilibrium is reached and the two temperatures are equal.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Relaxation time]
The \textbf{relaxation time} is the characteristic time required for two systems in contact to
reach thermal equilibrium. It varies enormously between systems --- from femtoseconds in dense
plasmas to geological timescales in some solids.
\end{definition}

Beyond thermal equilibrium, a system may also reach \textbf{mechanical equilibrium} (when all
unbalanced forces and pressure gradients have vanished), and \textbf{diffusive equilibrium} (when
the chemical potentials are equal so that no net flow of particles occurs). A system in all three
kinds of equilibrium is said to be in \emph{thermodynamic equilibrium}.

\begin{fact}[Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics]
If object $A$ is in thermal equilibrium with object $C$, and object $B$ is also in thermal
equilibrium with $C$, then $A$ and $B$ are in thermal equilibrium with each other. This
transitivity is what makes temperature a well-defined, measurable quantity; a full thermodynamic
treatment in terms of entropy is deferred to Chapter~3.
\end{fact}

\subsection{Kinetic Derivation of the Ideal Gas Law}

\begin{example}
We derive the ideal gas law from first principles by computing the pressure exerted by $N$ point
particles bouncing elastically inside a rectangular box of side length $L$ and volume $V = L^3$.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.85]

  \draw[gray!60] (2.5,0.5) -- (2,0);

  \draw[gray!60] (0.5,2.5) -- (0,2);
  % Front face of box
  \draw[thick] (0,0) -- (2,0) -- (2,2) -- (0,2) -- cycle;
  % Right wall highlighted
  \draw[thick, blue!70!black, line width=2pt] (2,0) -- (2,2);
  % Depth edges
  \draw[gray!60] (0,0) -- (0.5,0.5);
  \draw[gray!60] (2,0) -- (2.5,0.5);
  \draw[gray!60] (2,2) -- (2.5,2.5);
  \draw[gray!60] (0,2) -- (0.5,2.5);
  % Particle
  \filldraw[black] (0.9,1.0) circle (0.08);
  % Velocity arrow
  \draw[->, thick, red] (0.9,1.0) -- (1.75,1.0) node[midway, above] {$v_x$};
  % Side length label
  \draw[<->] (0,-0.3) -- (2,-0.3) node[midway, below] {$L$};
\end{tikzpicture}\\[3pt]
{\small\itshape Particle with velocity $v_x$ bouncing elastically off the right wall of a box of side $L$.}
\end{center}

Consider a single particle with velocity component $v_{xi}$ directed toward one wall. Upon
elastic reflection only the normal component reverses, so the momentum transferred to the wall
per collision is $\Delta p_{xi} = 2mv_{xi}$. The particle takes a time $\Delta t = 2L/v_{xi}$ to
travel to the opposite wall and return, giving a time-averaged force from the $i$-th particle of
\begin{equation}
    F_{xi} = \frac{\Delta p_{xi}}{\Delta t} = \frac{2mv_{xi}}{2L/v_{xi}} = \frac{mv_{xi}^2}{L}.
\end{equation}
Summing over all $N$ particles, the total force on the wall is
\begin{equation}
    F_x = \frac{m}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{N} v_{xi}^2 = \frac{mN\langle v_x^2\rangle}{L}.
\end{equation}
Because the gas is isotropic, there is no preferred direction, so
$\langle v_x^2\rangle = \langle v_y^2\rangle = \langle v_z^2\rangle \equiv \langle v^2\rangle$.
The pressure $P = F_x/L^2$ is therefore
\begin{equation}
    P = \frac{mN\langle v^2\rangle}{V}.
\end{equation}
Introducing temperature through $k_BT \equiv m\langle v^2\rangle$ --- which identifies temperature
with mean kinetic energy per degree of freedom, consistent with the equipartition theorem
discussed in Section~\ref{sec:equipartition} --- this becomes

\begin{formula}[Ideal Gas Law]
\begin{equation}
    PV = Nk_BT \qquad \text{or equivalently} \qquad PV = nRT,
\end{equation}
where $n$ is the number of moles, $R = 8.31\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ is the universal gas
constant, and $k_B = R/N_A = 1.38\times10^{-23}\,\mathrm{J/K}$ is Boltzmann's constant.
\end{formula}

The average internal energy of an ideal gas follows immediately:
$\langle U\rangle = \tfrac{3}{2}Nk_BT$. This energy is entirely kinetic (the particles do not
interact), it is extensive in $N$, and temperature is intensive.
\end{example}

\subsection{The Barometric Formula}

\begin{example}
The ideal gas law has a direct application in the vertical structure of the atmosphere. Consider
a thin horizontal slab of air at height $z$, thickness $dz$, and cross-sectional area $A$.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.85]
  % Slab rectangle
  \fill[gray!20] (0,1.0) rectangle (3.0,1.8);
  \draw[thick] (0,1.0) rectangle (3.0,1.8);
  % Area label
  \node at (1.5,1.4) {$A$};
  % Thickness label
  \draw[<->] (3.3,1.0) -- (3.3,1.8) node[midway, right] {$dz$};
  % Height label
  \draw[<->] (-0.5,0) -- (-0.5,1.0) node[midway, left] {$z$};
  \draw[dashed, gray] (0,0) -- (-0.3,0);
  \draw[dashed, gray] (0,1.0) -- (-0.3,1.0);
  % Ground line
  \draw[thick] (-0.3,0) -- (3.3,0);
  % P(z) arrow upward from below
  \draw[->, thick, blue!70!black, line width=1.5pt] (1.5,0.3) -- (1.5,1.0)
    node[midway, right] {$P(z)\,A$};
  % P(z+dz) arrow downward from above
  \draw[->, thick, red!70!black, line width=1.5pt] (1.5,2.5) -- (1.5,1.8)
    node[midway, right] {$P(z{+}dz)\,A$};
  % Weight arrow
  \draw[->, thick, black!70, line width=1.5pt] (0.6,1.8) -- (0.6,1.0)
    node[midway, left] {$\rho g A\,dz$};
\end{tikzpicture}\\[3pt]
{\small\itshape Force balance on an atmospheric slab of area $A$ and thickness $dz$ at height $z$.}
\end{center}

Vertical force balance on the slab gives $P(z)\,A = P(z+dz)\,A + \rho g\,A\,dz$, i.e.\
$dP/dz = -\rho g$. Using the ideal gas law in the form $\rho = mP/(k_BT)$, where $m$ is the mean
molecular mass:
\begin{equation}
    \dv{P}{z} = -\frac{mg}{k_BT}\,P.
\end{equation}
At constant temperature this separable ODE integrates to the

\begin{formula}[Barometric Formula]
\begin{equation}
    P(z) = P(0)\,e^{-mgz/(k_BT)}.
\end{equation}
\end{formula}

Pressure falls exponentially with altitude, with a characteristic scale height $H = k_BT/(mg)$.
For air with mean molecular mass $\bar{M} \approx 29\,\mathrm{g/mol}$ at $T = 300\,\mathrm{K}$,
the scale height is approximately $8.5\,\mathrm{km}$, consistent with the observed thinning of
the atmosphere.
\end{example}

\section{The Van der Waals Gas}
% ============================================================

Real gases deviate from the ideal gas law because molecules have a finite size and interact with
one another. At short range the interaction is strongly repulsive; at larger separations it is
weakly attractive. The Van der Waals model captures both effects with two phenomenological
parameters $a$ and $b$.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.85]
  \draw[->] (0,-1.6) -- (0,2.8) node[above] {$V(r)$};
  \draw[->] (0,0) -- (5.2,0) node[right] {$r$};
  % Lennard-Jones-like curve: repulsive wall, well near r=R, approaches 0
  \draw[thick, blue!80!black, domain=0.72:4.8, samples=120]
    plot (\x, { 0.8/(\x-0.5)^2 - 1.2/(\x+0.1) + 0.18 });
  % Zero dashed line
  \draw[dashed, gray] (0,0) -- (5.0,0);
  % Mark well minimum near r=R (approximately x=1.4)
  \draw[dashed, gray] (1.55,-1.25) -- (1.55,0);
  \node[below] at (1.55,0) {$R$};
  % Well depth annotation
  \draw[<->] (2.4,-1.25) -- (2.4,0) node[midway, right] {\small well depth};
  \draw[dashed, gray] (1.55,-1.25) -- (2.4,-1.25);
  % Label approaching zero
  \node[right, font=\small] at (4.2,0.15) {$V\to 0$};
\end{tikzpicture}\\[3pt]
{\small\itshape Lennard-Jones-type intermolecular potential: hard-core repulsion at short range, attractive well near $r=R$.}
\end{center}

\textbf{Excluded volume.} Each molecule excludes other molecules from a volume $b$ around itself.
With $N$ molecules in the container, the effective volume available to any one molecule is reduced
from $V$ to $V - Nb$.

\textbf{Attractive interactions.} A molecule near the wall of the container is pulled back toward
the interior by its neighbours. The number of neighbours within range scales as $N/V$, and the
probability that each such neighbour is itself in a region of high density also scales as $N/V$,
so the total cohesive potential energy scales as $(N/V)^2$. This reduces the effective pressure
by $aN^2/V^2$.

Substituting both corrections into the ideal gas law --- replacing $V$ by $V - Nb$ and $P$ by
$P + aN^2/V^2$ --- yields the

\begin{formula}[Van der Waals Equation of State]
\begin{equation}
    \left(P + \frac{aN^2}{V^2}\right)(V - Nb) = Nk_BT.
\end{equation}
\end{formula}

In the dilute limit $Nb \ll V$ and $aN^2/V^2 \ll P$, the Van der Waals equation reduces to the
ideal gas law.

\section{The Equipartition Theorem}
\label{sec:equipartition}
% ============================================================

\begin{theorem}[Equipartition Theorem]
In classical thermal equilibrium at temperature $T$, the ensemble average of any energy term that
is quadratic in a coordinate or momentum equals $\tfrac{1}{2}k_BT$. That is, for any degree of
freedom $q$ with energy contribution $\tfrac{1}{2}\alpha q^2$,
\begin{equation}
    \left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\alpha q^2\right\rangle = \tfrac{1}{2}k_BT.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

The theorem applies to translational kinetic energies $\tfrac{1}{2}mv_x^2$,
$\tfrac{1}{2}mv_y^2$, $\tfrac{1}{2}mv_z^2$; to rotational kinetic energies
$\tfrac{1}{2}I\omega^2$; and to both the kinetic and potential energies of a vibrational mode
$\tfrac{1}{2}\mu\dot{r}^2$ and $\tfrac{1}{2}kr^2$. Each such quadratic term is a separate degree
of freedom, and each contributes $\tfrac{1}{2}k_BT$ to the mean energy independently.

\textbf{Counting degrees of freedom.} A monatomic ideal gas has $f = 3$ translational degrees of
freedom. A diatomic molecule has 3 translational, 2 rotational (the rotation along the bond axis
is frozen out quantum mechanically because the moment of inertia about that axis is negligibly
small), and 2 vibrational degrees (one kinetic, one potential), giving $f = 7$ classically.
However, at room temperature the vibrational modes of most diatomic gases are not thermally
activated, so in practice $f = 5$.

\end{center}

Quantum mechanics freezes out a mode when $k_BT \ll \hbar\omega$ for that mode, so the effective
$f$ rises with temperature as successive modes become thermally accessible. This is why measured
heat capacities of diatomic gases are temperature-dependent, transitioning from $f = 3$ at very
low temperatures, through $f = 5$ at room temperature, toward $f = 7$ at very high temperatures.

If $f$ denotes the total number of active quadratic degrees of freedom per particle, the total
thermal energy of $N$ particles is

\begin{formula}
\begin{equation}
    U_{\text{thermal}} = \frac{f}{2}Nk_BT.
\end{equation}
\end{formula}

Differentiating with respect to temperature at constant volume gives the heat capacity
$C_V = \tfrac{f}{2}Nk_B$. For a crystalline solid, each atom participates in $3N$ vibrational
normal modes, each contributing 2 quadratic terms, giving $f = 6$ per atom and

\begin{theorem}[Dulong--Petit Law]
\begin{equation}
    U = 3Nk_BT, \qquad C_V = 3Nk_B = 3nR.
\end{equation}
This prediction is in good agreement with measured room-temperature heat capacities of many
elemental solids. It fails at low temperatures where quantum mechanics suppresses the vibrational
modes, a regime treated by the Einstein and Debye models.
\end{theorem}

\section{Heat, Work, and the First Law}
% ============================================================

\begin{definition}[Heat and Work]
\textbf{Heat} is any spontaneous flow of energy from one object to another caused by a temperature
difference. \textbf{Work} is any transfer of energy other than heat. Both are processes (energy
in transit), not properties of a state.
\end{definition}

Heat can be transferred by three mechanisms. \textbf{Conduction} is the transfer of thermal energy
through direct molecular contact. \textbf{Convection} is driven by bulk motion of a fluid: warmer
material expands, becomes less dense, and rises, continuously transporting energy. \textbf{Radiation}
is the emission and absorption of electromagnetic waves and requires no material medium.

\end{tikzpicture}\\[3pt]
{\small\itshape Spontaneous heat flow from hot to cold by conduction.}
\end{center}

\begin{theorem}[First Law of Thermodynamics]
For a closed system, the change in internal energy equals the sum of heat added to the system and
work done on the system:
\begin{equation}
    dU = \dbar Q + \dbar W.
\end{equation}
The internal energy $U$ is a \emph{state function}: its differential is exact, and the integral
$\Delta U = U_f - U_i$ depends only on the endpoints. Heat $\dbar Q$ and work $\dbar W$ are
\emph{inexact} differentials --- they are not the differentials of any state function, and their
integrals over a process depend on the path taken, not merely on the endpoints.
\end{theorem}

The path-dependence of $\dbar Q$ and $\dbar W$ is the thermodynamic instance of the mathematical
result discussed in Chapter~1: for an inexact differential, the loop integral
$\oint \vec{V}\cdot d\vec{x}$ is in general non-zero (it equals the surface integral of the curl).
In integrated form, the First Law says that for any process $\Delta U = Q + W$, where $Q$ and $W$
are the total heat absorbed and total work done on the system, respectively.

\end{tikzpicture}\\[3pt]
{\small\itshape Quasistatic compression: piston of area $A$ moves inward by $dx$, reducing volume by $dV=A\,dx$.}
\end{center}

For a \textbf{quasistatic} process --- one slow enough that the gas remains in equilibrium at
every instant and the pressure is spatially uniform --- the force on the piston equals $F = PA$.
Moving the piston inward by $dx$ decreases the volume by $dV = -A\,dx$, giving

\begin{formula}[Compression Work]
\begin{equation}
    W = -\int_{V_i}^{V_f} P(V)\,dV.
\end{equation}
\end{formula}

When the gas is compressed ($V_f < V_i$), this integral is negative so $W > 0$: positive work is
done on the gas. When the gas expands, it does positive work on its surroundings ($W < 0$). For a
non-quasistatic process, the pressure is not well-defined throughout, and one cannot evaluate the
integral; only $\Delta U = U_f - U_i$ can be computed from the endpoint state functions.

\subsection{Heat Capacities}

\begin{definition}[Heat Capacity]
The heat capacity $C$ is the heat required to raise the temperature of a system by $1\,\mathrm{K}$:
$C = Q/\Delta T$. The specific heat capacity is $c = C/m$.
\end{definition}

Since $Q$ depends on the process, the heat capacity is not uniquely defined without specifying
the conditions. The two canonical cases are:

\textbf{Constant volume.} No work is done ($dV = 0$, $\dbar W = 0$), so the First Law gives
$\dbar Q = dU$ and

\begin{formula}[Heat Capacity at Constant Volume]
\begin{equation}
    C_V = \left.\pdv{U}{T}\right|_V.
\end{equation}
\end{formula}

For an ideal gas with $f$ active degrees of freedom, $C_V = \tfrac{f}{2}Nk_B$.

\textbf{Constant pressure.} The gas is free to expand when heated and does work on its
surroundings. Starting from $\dbar Q = dU + P\,dV$ (First Law with $\dbar W = -P\,dV$):
\begin{equation}
    C_P = \left.\pdv{U}{T}\right|_P + P\left.\pdv{V}{T}\right|_P.
\end{equation}
For an ideal gas, $(\partial U/\partial V)_T = 0$ (internal energy depends only on temperature)
and $(\partial V/\partial T)_P = Nk_B/P$, so

\begin{formula}[$C_P$ for an Ideal Gas (Mayer's Relation)]
\begin{equation}
    C_P = C_V + Nk_B = C_V + nR.
\end{equation}
\end{formula}

The extra $Nk_B$ accounts for the work of expansion against the atmosphere: some of the heat added
at constant pressure goes into pushing back the surroundings rather than raising the temperature.
Equivalently, since $dH = \dbar Q$ at constant pressure, one has $C_P = (\partial H/\partial T)_P$.
The dimensionless ratio $\gamma = C_P/C_V = (f+2)/f$ is the adiabatic index.

\subsection{Latent Heat}

During a phase transition (melting, boiling), a system absorbs heat without any change in
temperature. The ordinary definition $C = Q/\Delta T$ would give an infinite heat capacity,
signalling that temperature is the wrong variable here.

\begin{definition}[Latent Heat]
The latent heat $L$ is the total heat required to convert a substance completely from one phase
to another at constant pressure. The specific latent heat is $\ell = L/m$, measured in
$\mathrm{J/kg}$.
\end{definition}

The energy absorbed during a phase transition goes into breaking intermolecular bonds rather than
increasing molecular kinetic energies, which is why the temperature remains constant throughout
the transition. Representative values: melting ice, $\ell = 333\,\mathrm{J/g}$; boiling water,
$\ell = 2260\,\mathrm{J/g}$.

% ============================================================
\section{Thermodynamic Processes}
% ============================================================

A quasi-static transformation moves the system along a definite path in state space; the path is
called a \textbf{process}. For any such process, the total work and heat are found by integrating
$\dbar W$ and $\dbar Q$ along the path, while $\Delta U = U_f - U_i$ is path-independent.

\subsection{Isothermal Compression}

An isothermal process is carried out slowly enough that the gas remains in thermal contact with a
reservoir and its temperature stays constant. With $T$ fixed and $PV = Nk_BT = \text{const}$:

\begin{formula}[Isothermal Work, Ideal Gas]
\begin{equation}
    W = -\int_{V_i}^{V_f}\frac{Nk_BT}{V}\,dV = Nk_BT\ln\frac{V_i}{V_f}.
\end{equation}
\end{formula}

Since $\Delta T = 0$, the internal energy is unchanged ($\Delta U = 0$), and the First Law gives
$Q = -W$: all the work done on the gas flows out as heat to the reservoir.

\subsection{Adiabatic Compression}

An adiabatic process is carried out so rapidly that no heat is exchanged: $\dbar Q = 0$. With
$\dbar W = -P\,dV$ and $dU = C_V\,dT$, the First Law becomes
\begin{equation}
    \frac{f}{2}Nk_B\,dT = -\frac{Nk_BT}{V}\,dV
    \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
    \frac{f}{2}\frac{dT}{T} = -\frac{dV}{V}.
\end{equation}
Integrating and exponentiating yields

\begin{formula}[Adiabatic Constraints]
\begin{equation}
    VT^{f/2} = \text{const}, \qquad\qquad PV^{\gamma} = \text{const}, \qquad \gamma = \frac{f+2}{f} = \frac{C_P}{C_V}.
\end{equation}
\end{formula}

The $PV^\gamma = \text{const}$ form is obtained by substituting $T = PV/(Nk_B)$ into the first
relation. Since $\gamma > 1$, the adiabat $P \propto V^{-\gamma}$ is steeper than the isotherm
$P \propto V^{-1}$ in the $P$--$V$ diagram: compressing the gas adiabatically raises its
temperature (no heat escapes), causing a faster pressure rise.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.85]
  \draw[->] (0,0) -- (4.5,0) node[right] {$V$};
  \draw[->] (0,0) -- (0,4.0) node[above] {$P$};
  % Initial point at (Vi, Pi) = (3.5, 0.9)
  % Final volume Vf = 1.3
  % Isotherm: P*V = const => P = 3.15/V  (passes through (3.5,0.9))
  \draw[thick, blue!70!black, domain=1.1:3.8, samples=80]
    plot (\x, {3.15/\x}) node[right, font=\small] {Isotherm $P\propto V^{-1}$};
  % Adiabat: P*V^gamma = const, gamma=5/3 => P = 3.15 * 3.5^(2/3) / V^(5/3)
  % At V=3.5: P=0.9 (same start). Constant = 0.9*3.5^(5/3) = 0.9*8.02 = 7.22
  \draw[thick, red!70!black, domain=1.1:3.8, samples=80]
    plot (\x, {7.22/(\x^1.667)}) node[right, font=\small] {Adiabat $P\propto V^{-\gamma}$};
  % Mark initial point
  \filldraw (3.5,0.9) circle (0.07) node[above right, font=\small] {$(V_i,P_i)$};
  % Mark Vf dashed line
  \draw[dashed, gray] (1.3,0) node[below, font=\small] {$V_f$} -- (1.3,3.5);

  \filldraw[blue!70!black] (1.3, {3.15/1.3}) circle (0.06);

\end{center}

The work done on the gas in an adiabatic process is purely the change in internal energy
($Q = 0$):
\begin{equation}
    W_{\text{adiabat}} = \Delta U = \frac{f}{2}Nk_B(T_f - T_i)
    = \frac{Nk_BT_i}{\gamma - 1}\!\left[\!\left(\frac{V_f}{V_i}\right)^{1-\gamma} - 1\right].
\end{equation}

\subsection{Isochoric and Isobaric Processes}

In an \textbf{isochoric} (constant-volume) process, $dV = 0$, no work is done, and the First Law
reduces to $dU = \dbar Q$. All heat added goes entirely into raising the internal energy:
$\Delta U = \int C_V\,dT$.

In an \textbf{isobaric} (constant-pressure) process, the gas is free to expand. Work is done on
the surroundings: $\Delta W = -P\Delta V$, and the heat absorbed is $\Delta Q = \int C_P\,dT$.
On a $P$--$V$ diagram, the isobar is a horizontal line; the area below it between $V_i$ and $V_f$
gives the work done by the gas.

% ============================================================
\section{Enthalpy}
% ============================================================

Rather than tracking the internal energy alone, it is often convenient to account simultaneously
for the work required to make room for a system against a constant external pressure. This
motivates the definition of enthalpy.

\begin{definition}[Enthalpy]
The enthalpy of a system is
\begin{equation}
    H \coloneq U + PV.
\end{equation}
Physically, $H$ represents the total energy cost to create the system from nothing: $U$ to
assemble its internal energy, plus the work $PV$ done against the atmosphere to carve out the
space it occupies.
\end{definition}

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.85]
  % Atmosphere region (background)
  \fill[blue!8] (-0.5,-0.3) rectangle (5.5,2.3);
  \node[font=\small, gray] at (4.5,1.8) {atmosphere $P$};
  % Container (open box: left, bottom, right walls)
  \fill[white] (1.2,0) rectangle (3.8,2.0);
  \draw[very thick] (1.2,0) -- (1.2,2.0);
  \draw[very thick] (1.2,0) -- (3.8,0);
  \draw[very thick] (3.8,0) -- (3.8,2.0);
  % Dashed top (open top or being pushed in)
  \draw[dashed, thick] (1.2,2.0) -- (3.8,2.0);
  % Volume label inside
  \node[font=\small] at (2.5,1.0) {Volume $V$};
  % Arrow showing atmosphere pressing (from right)
  \draw[->, very thick, red!70!black] (4.8,1.0) -- (3.8,1.0)
    node[midway, above, font=\small] {$W = PV$};
  \node[right, font=\small, red!70!black] at (4.8,1.0) {$P$};
\end{tikzpicture}\\[3pt]
{\small\itshape Enthalpy includes the work $PV$ done against atmosphere to make room for the system.}
\end{center}

\subsection{Why Enthalpy is Convenient}

Taking the differential of $H = U + PV$ and substituting the First Law
$dU = \dbar Q - P\,dV + \dbar W_{\text{other}}$:
\begin{equation}
    dH = dU + P\,dV + V\,dP = \dbar Q + V\,dP + \dbar W_{\text{other}}.
\end{equation}
At constant pressure ($dP = 0$) with no other work, the compression bookkeeping drops out:

\begin{formula}
\begin{equation}
    \Delta H = Q \qquad (\text{constant pressure, no other work}).
\end{equation}
\end{formula}

Whenever a process occurs at constant pressure --- combustion, chemical reactions in open vessels,
boiling or condensation --- the heat exchanged equals the enthalpy change, with no need to track
the $P\Delta V$ expansion work separately. The more general statement is $\Delta H = Q + W_{\text{other}}$,
where $W_{\text{other}}$ is any work beyond compression (electrical work, shaft work, etc.).

\subsection{Heat Capacity at Constant Pressure}

Because $dH = \dbar Q$ at constant pressure, the heat capacity at constant pressure is simply

\begin{formula}
\begin{equation}
    C_P = \left.\pdv{H}{T}\right|_P.
\end{equation}
\end{formula}

This makes the roles of $U$ and $H$ transparent: $U$ is the natural thermodynamic potential for
constant-volume processes ($C_V = (\partial U/\partial T)_V$), while $H$ is the natural potential
for constant-pressure processes ($C_P = (\partial H/\partial T)_P$).

\subsection{Enthalpy of Formation}

\begin{example}
Standard enthalpy tables record the heat released or absorbed when compounds are formed from
their constituent elements at constant pressure ($1\,\mathrm{atm}$, $298\,\mathrm{K}$). For
example, the formation of liquid water,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{H_2} + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{O_2} \longrightarrow \mathrm{H_2O}\,(\ell),
\end{equation}
has a standard enthalpy of formation $\Delta H = -286\,\mathrm{kJ/mol}$: forming one mole of
liquid water releases $286\,\mathrm{kJ}$. Most of this energy comes from the internal chemical
energy of the bonds; a small portion arises from the volume change as the gaseous reactants are
consumed and the atmosphere does work to fill the vacated space.

Boiling water at $373\,\mathrm{K}$ and $1\,\mathrm{atm}$ requires
$\Delta H = 40{,}660\,\mathrm{J/mol}$. Of this, $PV = RT \approx 3100\,\mathrm{J/mol}$ goes
into pushing the atmosphere back as steam occupies roughly $1600$ times the liquid volume,
representing about $8\%$ of the total enthalpy of vaporisation.
\end{example}

\section{Rates of Processes}
% ============================================================

The preceding sections described what happens in thermodynamic processes. This section addresses
how fast energy and matter are transported.

\subsection{Heat Conduction and Fourier's Law}

Heat flows spontaneously from hot regions to cold. Consider a slab of material of area $A$ and
thickness $\Delta x$ separating a warm interior at temperature $T_2$ from a cooler exterior at
$T_1 < T_2$.

\begin{center}

  \node[font=\small, align=center] at (3.35,1.2) {Cold\\$T_1$};

  \draw[thick] (0,0) -- (0,2.4);

  \draw[thick] (0,2.4) -- (4.0,2.4);
  \draw[thick] (4.0,0) -- (4.0,2.4);

  \draw[<->] (1.3,-0.4) -- (2.7,-0.4) node[midway, below, font=\small] {$\Delta x$};

    node[midway, above, font=\small] {$Q/\Delta t$};
\end{tikzpicture}\\[3pt]
{\small\itshape Fourier's law: heat flux through a slab of thickness $\Delta x$ is proportional to the temperature gradient.}
\end{center}

By dimensional reasoning, the heat flow should scale with area, temperature gradient, and elapsed
time, and depend on a material constant. This yields

\begin{formula}[Fourier's Law of Heat Conduction]
\begin{equation}
    \frac{Q}{\Delta t} = -k_t\,A\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial x},
\end{equation}
where $k_t$ is the \textbf{thermal conductivity} of the material. The negative sign ensures heat
flows in the direction of decreasing temperature.
\end{formula}

Thermal conductivities span four orders of magnitude across common materials (in
$\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}K^{-1}}$): air ($0.026$), wood ($0.08$), water ($0.6$), glass ($0.8$), iron
($80$), copper ($400$). Metals conduct heat efficiently because free electrons, which carry energy
far more readily than lattice phonons, dominate the transport.

\subsection{Thermal Conductivity from Kinetic Theory}

In a gas, heat is transported by molecules carrying kinetic energy from hotter to cooler regions.
The key length scale is the \textbf{mean free path} $\ell$ --- the average distance a molecule
travels between successive collisions.

\begin{definition}[Mean Free Path]
Treating each molecule as a sphere of radius $r$, two molecules collide when their centres come
within $2r$. The moving molecule sweeps an effective cylinder of radius $2r$; a collision becomes
likely when the cylinder volume equals the average volume per molecule:
\begin{equation}
    \ell \approx \frac{1}{4\pi r^2}\cdot\frac{V}{N}.
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.85]
  % Draw the cylinder (shown as an ellipse + rectangle for 3D effect)
  % Cylinder axis is horizontal, radius 2r, length ell
  % Front ellipse at right end
  \fill[blue!12] (4.5,1.0) ellipse (0.22 and 0.7);
  \draw[thick] (4.5,1.0) ellipse (0.22 and 0.7);
  % Top and bottom edges of cylinder
  \draw[thick] (0.5, 1.7) -- (4.5, 1.7);
  \draw[thick] (0.5, 0.3) -- (4.5, 0.3);
  % Fill interior
  \fill[blue!8] (0.5,0.3) rectangle (4.5,1.7);
  \draw[dashed, blue!60] (0.5,1.0) ellipse (0.22 and 0.7);
  % Cross-section circle at left (the collision cross section)
  \fill[gray!30] (0.5,1.0) ellipse (0.22 and 0.7);
  \draw[thick] (0.5,1.0) ellipse (0.22 and 0.7);
  % Label 2r on the cross section
  \draw[<->] (0.5,1.0) -- (0.5,1.7) node[midway, left, font=\small] {$2r$};
  % Length label
  \draw[<->] (0.5,2.2) -- (4.5,2.2) node[midway, above, font=\small] {$\ell$};
  % Moving molecule (dot at right face center)
  \filldraw[red!70!black] (4.5,1.0) circle (0.09);
  % Velocity arrow
  \draw[->, thick, red!70!black] (4.5,1.0) -- (5.5,1.0) node[right, font=\small] {$\bar{v}$};
  % Label swept volume
  \node[font=\small, blue!70!black] at (2.5,1.0) {swept volume};
\end{tikzpicture}\\[3pt]
{\small\itshape Mean free path $\ell$: the cylinder of radius $2r$ swept before a collision becomes likely.}
\end{center}

To estimate $k_t$, consider two adjacent slabs of gas separated by an imaginary barrier. In one
mean-free-time $\overline{\Delta t} = \ell/\bar{v}$, roughly half the molecules in each slab
cross the barrier and carry their thermal energy with them. The net heat transferred from the
hotter slab to the cooler one is
\begin{equation}
    Q \approx -\tfrac{1}{2}\,C_V\,(T_2 - T_1) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\,C_V\,\ell\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}.
\end{equation}
Comparing with Fourier's law and using $C_V/V = \tfrac{f}{2}Nk_B/V = \tfrac{f}{2}P/T$ (from the
ideal gas law):

\begin{formula}[Thermal Conductivity, Kinetic Theory]
\begin{equation}
    k_t = \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{C_V}{V}\,\bar{v}\,\ell = \frac{f}{4}\,\frac{P}{T}\,\bar{v}\,\ell.
\end{equation}
\end{formula}

Since $\bar{v} \propto T^{1/2}$ and $\ell \propto T/P$ (from the ideal gas law), the product
$\bar{v}\,\ell \propto T^{3/2}/P$ and $k_t \propto T^{1/2}$, independent of pressure. This
pressure-independence is confirmed experimentally over a wide range and is a non-trivial prediction
of kinetic theory.

\subsection{Diffusion and Fick's Laws}

While heat conduction transports energy, \textbf{diffusion} transports particles themselves, driven
by a gradient in number density $n$ (particles per unit volume). The \textbf{flux} $J_x$ is the
net number of particles crossing a unit area per unit time.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.85]
  % Outer box
  \draw[thick] (0,0) rectangle (5.0,2.4);
  % Dividing line
  \draw[dashed, gray] (2.5,0) -- (2.5,2.4);
  % Left half label (high concentration) with dots
  \node[font=\small, above] at (1.25,2.4) {high $n$};
  % Many dots on left
  \foreach \x/\y in {0.35/0.4, 0.8/1.1, 0.5/1.8, 1.1/0.7, 1.5/1.5,
                      0.7/2.0, 1.2/2.1, 1.8/0.3, 2.0/1.0, 1.9/1.9,
                      0.4/1.3, 1.6/0.9, 0.9/0.5, 1.3/1.2, 2.1/0.6}{
    \filldraw[black] (\x,\y) circle (0.06);
  }
  % Right half label (low concentration) with fewer dots
  \node[font=\small, above] at (3.75,2.4) {low $n$};
  % Fewer dots on right
  \foreach \x/\y in {2.8/1.2, 3.5/0.5, 4.2/1.8, 3.0/2.0, 4.6/0.8}{
    \filldraw[black] (\x,\y) circle (0.06);
  }
  % Net flux arrow
  \draw[->, very thick, red!70!black] (2.5,1.2) -- (3.8,1.2)
    node[right, font=\small] {$J_x$};
\end{tikzpicture}\\[3pt]
{\small\itshape Diffusion: net particle flux $J_x$ flows down the concentration gradient.}
\end{center}

\begin{formula}[Fick's First Law]
\begin{equation}
    J_x = -D\,\frac{\partial n}{\partial x},
\end{equation}
where $D$ is the \textbf{diffusion coefficient}. The negative sign ensures flux is directed down
the concentration gradient.
\end{formula}

Combining Fick's First Law with conservation of particle number
($\partial n/\partial t = -\partial J_x/\partial x$) gives

\begin{formula}[Fick's Second Law]
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial n}{\partial t} = D\,\frac{\partial^2 n}{\partial x^2}.
\end{equation}
\end{formula}

This diffusion equation predicts that an initially sharp concentration profile spreads out over
time with a characteristic width growing as $\sqrt{Dt}$. By the same kinetic-theory argument as
above, the diffusion coefficient scales as

\begin{formula}
\begin{equation}
    D \sim \bar{v}\,\ell,
\end{equation}
\end{formula}

so the same mean free path governs both energy transport (thermal conductivity) and particle
transport (diffusion). The ratio $D\rho c_P/k_t$ --- the Lewis number --- is of order unity for
gases, reflecting this shared microscopic origin.
