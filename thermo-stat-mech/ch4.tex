\chapter{The Laws of Thermodynamics and Heat Engines}

\section{The Second Law and Irreversibility}

\subsection{Macroscopic Statement}

Any isolated system will almost certainly be found in or near the macrostate of greatest multiplicity. Fluctuations away from this peak are exponentially suppressed. This is the statistical underpinning of the second law.

\begin{theorem}[Second Law of Thermodynamics]
The entropy of an isolated system never decreases:
\[
    \dv{S}{t} \geq 0.
\]
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Microscopic Origin: Phase-Space Argument}

The second law is not a consequence of any individual microscopic equation of motion (which is time-reversal invariant) but a statement about \emph{ensembles} of microstates. An initial macrostate corresponds to a compact blob in the $6N$-dimensional phase space. By Liouville's theorem, the blob's volume is conserved as it evolves, but it stretches and folds to overlap with an overwhelmingly larger number of macrostates --- entropy grows because of this increased overlap, not any change in volume.

\begin{example}
In free expansion, $N$ atoms confined to the left half of an insulated box expand to fill the whole box. No work is done and no heat is exchanged ($\Delta U = 0$), but each atom now has access to double the volume, so

\begin{formula}
\[
    \Delta S = k_B \ln \frac{\Omega_{\text{new}}}{\Omega_{\text{old}}} = N k_B \ln 2.
\]
\end{formula}

The ratio $\Omega_{\text{new}}/\Omega_{\text{old}} = 2^N$ makes spontaneous reversal (probability $2^{-N}$) utterly negligible for macroscopic $N$.
\end{example}

\begin{fact}
Isolated systems may only increase entropy. Fluctuations away from maximum entropy are suppressed by $e^{-\Delta S / k_B}$.
\end{fact}

\subsection{Ergodic Hypothesis}

\begin{definition}[Ergodic Hypothesis]
The time average of any macroscopic observable equals the ensemble average:
\[
    \langle f \rangle = \sum_{\{S_i\}} f(S_i)\, p(S_i) = \frac{1}{\tau}\int_0^\tau f(t)\,dt.
\]
\end{definition}

The ergodic hypothesis fails for glassy systems, systems with conserved charges partitioning phase space, and many-body localized systems. For a system in thermal equilibrium it holds to excellent approximation; for an isolated system in equilibrium every accessible microstate is equally likely, giving $S = k_B \ln \Omega$.

\subsection{Reversible and Irreversible Processes}

\begin{definition}
An \textbf{irreversible} process increases the total entropy of the universe; a \textbf{reversible} (quasi-static) process leaves it unchanged. A reversible process can be undone by an infinitesimally small change in external parameters, with the system remaining arbitrarily close to equilibrium throughout.
\end{definition}

Free expansion and heat flow across a finite temperature difference are irreversible. For a reversible infinitesimal process exchanging heat $\dbar Q$ at temperature $T$:
\begin{formula}
\[
    dS = \frac{\dbar Q}{T} \qquad (\text{reversible}).
\]
\end{formula}
For an irreversible process, $dS > \dbar Q / T$.

\subsection{The Clausius Inequality}

For a cyclic device, $\Delta S_\text{engine} = 0$ over a full cycle. The second law requires the total entropy change of the universe to be non-negative:

\begin{formula}[Clausius Inequality]
\[
    \oint \frac{\dbar Q}{T} \leq 0,
\]
with equality if and only if the cycle is reversible.
\end{formula}

\begin{example}
Heat $Q$ flowing from $T_H$ to $T_C < T_H$ gives
\[
    \Delta S_\text{univ} = Q\!\left(\frac{1}{T_C} - \frac{1}{T_H}\right) > 0,
\]
strictly positive for $T_C < T_H$. As $dT \to 0$ the entropy production vanishes.
\end{example}

\section{Entropy Changes in Thermodynamic Processes}

\subsection{General Formula}

From the fundamental identity $dU = T\,dS - p\,dV$:
\begin{formula}
\[
    dS = \frac{1}{T}\,dU + \frac{p}{T}\,dV.
\]
\end{formula}
For an ideal gas ($dU = C_V\,dT$, $p = Nk_B T / V$):
\[
    dS = \frac{C_V\,dT}{T} + \frac{Nk_B}{V}\,dV.
\]

\subsection{Isochoric (Constant-Volume) Heating}

\begin{formula}
\[
    \Delta S_\text{isochoric} = C_V \ln\frac{T_B}{T_A} = \frac{3}{2}Nk_B \ln\frac{T_B}{T_A}.
\]
\end{formula}
No work is done; $\Delta Q = \Delta U = C_V(T_B - T_A)$.

\subsection{Isobaric (Constant-Pressure) Heating}

\begin{formula}
\[
    \Delta S_\text{isobaric} = C_P \ln\frac{T_B}{T_A} = \frac{5}{2}Nk_B\ln\frac{T_B}{T_A}.
\]
\end{formula}
Since $C_P > C_V$, $\Delta S_\text{isobaric} > \Delta S_\text{isochoric}$ for the same temperature interval.

\subsection{Isothermal Expansion}

For an ideal gas at constant $T$, $\Delta U = 0$ and:
\begin{formula}
\[
    \Delta S_\text{isothermal} = Nk_B \ln\frac{V_B}{V_A}.
\]
\end{formula}
For a \emph{reversible} isothermal expansion the heat absorbed is $T\,\Delta S = Nk_BT\ln(V_B/V_A)$. For an \emph{irreversible} isothermal expansion (e.g.\ free expansion into vacuum), the same entropy increase occurs but $\Delta Q = 0$; the formula $\Delta S = Q/T$ does not apply to irreversible processes.

\subsection{Adiabatic Process}

For a reversible adiabatic process, $\dbar Q = 0$, so $dS = 0$: the process is isentropic. Because $S$ is a state function, $\oint dS = 0$ around any closed cycle.

\subsection{Reversible Heating}

Directly contacting a cold body at $T_C$ with a reservoir at $T_H > T_C$ is irreversible. Reversible heating requires a sequence of intermediate reservoirs at $T_C, T_C+dT, \ldots, T_H$; entropy production per step is $O((dT)^2/T^2) \to 0$.

\section{The Third Law}

\subsection{Three Equivalent Statements}

\begin{theorem}[Third Law of Thermodynamics]
The following statements are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item As $T \to 0$, the entropy of a perfect crystal approaches zero: $S \to 0$.
    \item As $T \to 0$, the heat capacity vanishes: $C_V \to 0$.
    \item It is impossible to cool a system to absolute zero in a finite number of processes.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

Statements 1 and 2 are linked by $S(T) = S(0) + \int_0^T C(T')/T'\,dT'$: convergence of this integral as $T' \to 0$ requires $C(T') \to 0$. Statement 3 follows because as $T \to 0$ each isothermal step can remove only a vanishingly small amount of entropy, so each subsequent adiabatic temperature drop also vanishes --- absolute zero is approached only asymptotically.

\subsection{Physical Origin: Ground-State Degeneracy and Residual Entropy}

For a perfect crystal at $T \to 0$, $\Omega = 1$ and $S = k_B \ln 1 = 0$. If the ground state has degeneracy $g$, then $S \to k_B \ln g$. Many real systems exhibit \textbf{residual entropy} --- nonzero entropy at low temperature from degrees of freedom (isotope disorder, frozen molecular orientations, random nuclear spins) that cannot equilibrate on laboratory timescales and are quenched without contributing to $C_V$. The vanishing of $C_V$ at low $T$ reflects quantum degrees of freedom freezing out as $T$ drops below the gap $\hbar\omega$.

\subsection{Macroscopic View of Entropy}

The relation $dS = \dbar Q / T$ was Clausius's original definition of entropy. Energy flowing irreversibly from $T_H$ to $T_C$ carries entropy $\dbar Q / T_H$ out of the hot body but deposits $\dbar Q / T_C > \dbar Q / T_H$ into the cold body; net entropy creation per unit heat is $\dbar Q(1/T_C - 1/T_H) > 0$.

\section{Heat Engines and the Carnot Bound}
\label{sec:carnot}

\subsection{Definition of a Heat Engine}

\begin{definition}[Heat engine]
A heat engine operates cyclically, absorbing heat $Q_+$ from a reservoir at $T_+$, doing net work $W$, and rejecting waste heat $Q_-$ to a reservoir at $T_-$. Since $\oint dU = 0$, energy conservation gives $W = Q_+ - Q_-$.
\end{definition}

The net work per cycle equals the area enclosed in a $P$-$V$ diagram.

\begin{definition}[Thermal efficiency]
\[
    \eta = \frac{W}{Q_+} = 1 - \frac{Q_-}{Q_+}.
\]
\end{definition}

\textbf{Step 1 --- Isothermal expansion at $T_+$ (A $\to$ B).} At constant $T$, $\Delta U_1 = 0$, so
\[
    Q_+ = W_1 = Nk_B T_+ \ln\frac{V_B}{V_A} > 0; \qquad \Delta S_1 = \frac{Q_+}{T_+}.
\]

\textbf{Step 2 --- Adiabatic expansion (B $\to$ C).} $\Delta Q_2 = 0$, $dS = 0$; gas cools from $T_+$ to $T_-$ with $T_+ V_B^{\gamma-1} = T_- V_C^{\gamma-1}$.

\textbf{Step 3 --- Isothermal compression at $T_-$ (C $\to$ D).} $\Delta U_3 = 0$; heat $Q_- = Nk_B T_- \ln(V_C/V_D)$ is expelled to the cold reservoir.

\textbf{Step 4 --- Adiabatic compression (D $\to$ A).} $\Delta Q_4 = 0$, $dS = 0$; gas warms back to $T_+$ with $T_- V_D^{\gamma-1} = T_+ V_A^{\gamma-1}$.

\subsection{Full Ideal-Gas Derivation of the Carnot Efficiency}

Dividing the two adiabatic constraints gives $V_B/V_A = V_C/V_D$, so the two adiabatic work contributions cancel and the net work simplifies to

\begin{formula}
\[
    W_{\text{net}} = Nk_B(T_+ - T_-)\ln\frac{V_B}{V_A}.
\]
\end{formula}

Since $Q_+ = Nk_B T_+ \ln(V_B/V_A)$, the volume factors cancel:

\begin{formula}[Carnot efficiency]
\[
    \eta_\text{Carnot} = \frac{W_\text{net}}{Q_+} = 1 - \frac{T_-}{T_+}.
\]
\end{formula}

\begin{example}
With $T_+ = 600\ \text{K}$ and $T_- = 300\ \text{K}$: $\eta_\text{Carnot} = 50\%$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Efficiency from the Second Law}

Without specifying the working substance, $\Delta S_\text{engine} = 0$ per cycle, so the second law requires
\[
    \frac{Q_-}{T_-} \geq \frac{Q_+}{T_+} \implies \frac{Q_-}{Q_+} \geq \frac{T_-}{T_+},
\]
giving $\eta = 1 - Q_-/Q_+ \leq 1 - T_-/T_+ = \eta_\text{Carnot}$, with equality iff all heat exchange is reversible. In the $T$-$S$ diagram the reversibility condition $Q_+/T_+ = Q_-/T_-$ makes the cycle a rectangle.

\subsection{Carnot's Theorem: No Engine Can Exceed Carnot Efficiency}

\begin{theorem}[Carnot's Theorem]
No heat engine operating between $T_+$ and $T_-$ can have efficiency greater than $\eta_\text{Carnot} = 1 - T_-/T_+$. All reversible engines between the same two temperatures achieve exactly this efficiency.
\end{theorem}

Proof by contradiction: suppose engine $X$ has $\eta_X > \eta_\text{Carnot}$. Couple $X$ to a reversed Carnot refrigerator, using $X$'s work output to drive it, tuned so the refrigerator returns to the hot reservoir exactly the waste heat $Q_-$ that $X$ exhausts. The combined device then extracts net heat from the hot reservoir alone and converts it entirely into work --- a perpetual-motion machine of the second kind, violating the second law.

\section{Refrigerators and Heat Pumps}

Running the Carnot cycle in reverse: work $W_\text{in}$ is supplied, heat $Q_-$ is absorbed from the cold reservoir, and $Q_+ = Q_- + W_\text{in}$ is expelled to the hot reservoir.

\begin{definition}[COP of a refrigerator]
\[
    \text{COP}_\text{refrig} = \frac{Q_-}{W_\text{in}} = \frac{T_-}{T_+ - T_-} \quad \text{(ideal Carnot)}.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
For $T_+ \approx 308\ \text{K}$, $T_- \approx 278\ \text{K}$: ideal COP $\approx 9$, meaning a perfect refrigerator moves nine times as much thermal energy as the electrical work it consumes.
\end{example}

\begin{definition}[COP of a heat pump]
\[
    \text{COP}_\text{pump} = \frac{Q_+}{W_\text{in}} = \frac{T_+}{T_+ - T_-}.
\]
\end{definition}

Note $\text{COP}_\text{pump} = \text{COP}_\text{refrig} + 1 > 1$ always; a heat pump always delivers more thermal energy than the work it consumes.

\begin{example}
For $T_+ = 293\ \text{K}$, $T_- = 268\ \text{K}$: ideal COP $\approx 12$, substantially more efficient than direct electric heating.
\end{example}

In terms of Carnot efficiency: $\text{COP}_\text{refrig} = (1-\eta)/\eta$ and $\text{COP}_\text{pump} = 1/\eta$.

\section{The Stirling Engine}

\subsection{Four Steps of the Stirling Cycle}

The Stirling cycle replaces the Carnot's adiabatic legs with \emph{isochoric} (constant-volume) steps, operating between the same $T_+$ and $T_-$.

\textbf{Step 1} (isothermal expansion at $T_+$, $V_A \to V_C$): work $W_1 = Nk_BT_+\ln(V_C/V_A)$, same heat absorbed from hot reservoir.
\textbf{Step 2} (isochoric cooling $T_+ \to T_-$ at $V_C$): no work; gas releases $C_V(T_+ - T_-)$.
\textbf{Step 3} (isothermal compression at $T_-$, $V_C \to V_A$): heat $Q_- = Nk_BT_-\ln(V_C/V_A)$ expelled.
\textbf{Step 4} (isochoric heating $T_- \to T_+$ at $V_A$): no work; gas absorbs $C_V(T_+ - T_-)$.

\subsection{Net Work and Efficiency Without a Regenerator}

Net work comes only from the isothermal steps:
\[
    W_\text{net} = Nk_B(T_+ - T_-)\ln\frac{V_C}{V_A}.
\]
This equals the Carnot net work for the same temperatures and volume ratio, but the total heat input is larger since Step 4 must be fueled externally:
\[
    Q_\text{in} = Nk_BT_+\ln\frac{V_C}{V_A} + C_V(T_+ - T_-).
\]

\begin{formula}
\[
    \eta_\text{Stirling} = \frac{Nk_B(T_+-T_-)\ln(V_C/V_A)}{Nk_BT_+\ln(V_C/V_A) + C_V(T_+-T_-)} < \eta_\text{Carnot}.
\]
\end{formula}

\begin{example}
Despite lower efficiency, the Stirling engine can produce more net work per cycle than a Carnot engine at the same volume ratio, since Carnot's adiabatic legs limit the effective isothermal volume range. For $T_+/T_- = 2$, $V_C/V_A = 10$, $\gamma = 1.4$: $W_\text{Stirling}/W_\text{Carnot} \approx 4$.
\end{example}

\subsection{The Regenerator and Why $\eta_\text{Stirling} = \eta_\text{Carnot}$ with It}

The efficiency penalty arises entirely from the isochoric heat exchanges: Step 2 rejects $C_V(T_+ - T_-)$ while Step 4 requires the same amount. A \textbf{regenerator} --- a thermally massive medium (porous mesh or packed bed) between the hot and cold ends --- stores the heat rejected in Step 2 and returns it in Step 4, eliminating the need for external supply during isochoric heating.

With an ideal regenerator, the external heat input reduces to $Q_\text{in} = Nk_BT_+\ln(V_C/V_A)$, identical to the Carnot case.

\begin{theorem}[Stirling Engine with Perfect Regenerator]
A Stirling engine with a perfect regenerator achieves the Carnot efficiency:
\[
    \eta_\text{Stirling\,(with regenerator)} = 1 - \frac{T_-}{T_+} = \eta_\text{Carnot}.
\]
\end{theorem}

The regenerator internalises all isochoric heat exchange; from the perspective of the external reservoirs the cycle is identical to Carnot.
