\chapter{Quantum Statistics and the Grand Canonical Ensemble}

\section{Quantum Particles: Bosons and Fermions}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

A fundamental feature of quantum mechanics is that identical particles are
truly indistinguishable: there is no experiment that can track which electron
is which, or label one photon differently from another.  This indistinguishability
has deep consequences for many-body wavefunctions and for statistical mechanics.

\begin{definition}[Bosons and Fermions]
All particles in nature fall into one of two classes.  \textbf{Fermions} have
half-integer spin and their many-body wavefunction is \emph{antisymmetric} under
the exchange of any two particles.  \textbf{Bosons} have integer spin and their
many-body wavefunction is \emph{symmetric} under particle exchange.
\end{definition}

For two single-particle states $\psi_{k_1}$ and $\psi_{k_2}$, the properly
symmetrised two-particle wavefunctions read
\begin{align}
    \text{Bosons:}\quad
    \Psi_{k_1k_2}
    &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\!\left[\psi_{k_1}(1)\psi_{k_2}(2)
       +\psi_{k_2}(1)\psi_{k_1}(2)\right],\\
    \text{Fermions:}\quad
    \Psi_{k_1k_2}
    &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\!\left[\psi_{k_1}(1)\psi_{k_2}(2)
       -\psi_{k_2}(1)\psi_{k_1}(2)\right].
\end{align}
For fermions the wavefunction vanishes identically when $k_1 = k_2$: two
fermions cannot occupy the same quantum state.  This is the celebrated
\textbf{Pauli exclusion principle}.

\begin{fact}
For bosons the occupation number $n_k$ of any single-particle state $k$ can
be any non-negative integer, $n_k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$.  For fermions,
$n_k \in \{0, 1\}$ only.
\end{fact}

A general $N$-particle state is conveniently labelled in the
\emph{occupation number representation} as $|n_1, n_2, \ldots, n_k, \ldots\rangle$,
where $n_i$ counts the particles in single-particle state $i$ and the constraint
$\sum_i n_i = N$ is imposed.  The total energy and total particle number of such
a state are
\begin{equation}
    E = \sum_i \varepsilon_i n_i, \qquad N = \sum_i n_i.
\end{equation}

To appreciate why quantum statistics matters, consider the canonical partition
function at fixed $N$:
\begin{equation}
    Z_N = \sum_{\substack{n_1,n_2,\ldots \\ \sum_i n_i = N}}
    e^{-\beta n_1\varepsilon_1}\,e^{-\beta n_2\varepsilon_2}\cdots.
\end{equation}
Because the occupation numbers must satisfy $\sum_i n_i = N$, they are
\emph{coupled}, and the sum cannot be factored into independent single-mode
sums.  The familiar high-temperature approximation $Z_N \approx Z_1^N / N!$
is valid only when the typical occupancy of any state is much less than one,
so that the Pauli constraint is rarely triggered and bosonic enhancement is
negligible.  At lower temperatures, where occupancies of order unity become
common, we need a more powerful framework.

As a concrete illustration, consider three bosons distributed among harmonic
oscillator levels $k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$ with energies $(k + \tfrac{1}{2})\hbar\omega$.
The allowed occupation-number states are tuples $(n_0, n_1, n_2, \ldots)$ with
$\sum n_i = 3$.  At total energy $\tfrac{3}{2}\hbar\omega$ there is a single
state (all three in $k = 0$); at higher energies the number of states grows
rapidly as more ways of distributing excitations become available.  By contrast,
three spin-$\tfrac{1}{2}$ fermions cannot all occupy the lowest level; the
Pauli principle forces the lowest three available spin-states to be filled first.

\section{The Grand Canonical Ensemble}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

The natural way to handle quantum statistics is to abandon the fixed-$N$
constraint altogether.  We place the system in contact with a reservoir that
fixes both temperature $T$ and chemical potential $\mu$, allowing both energy
and particles to flow.

\begin{definition}[Grand Canonical Ensemble]
In the \textbf{grand canonical ensemble} the system exchanges both energy and
particles with a large reservoir characterised by temperature $T$ and chemical
potential $\mu$.  The macroscopic variables held fixed are $V$, $T$, and $\mu$;
the energy $U$ and particle number $N$ both fluctuate, with only their averages
$\langle U\rangle$ and $\langle N\rangle$ determined by the reservoir parameters.
\end{definition}

[DIAGRAM: Table comparing microcanonical ($V,N,U$ fixed; $T,P,\mu$ derived),
canonical ($V,N,T$ fixed; $U$ fluctuates), and grand canonical ($V,\mu,T$ fixed;
$U$ and $N$ fluctuate)]
\end{center}

\subsection{Derivation of the Grand Canonical Distribution}

The derivation follows the same logic as the canonical ensemble, extended to
include particle exchange.  Consider a small system in contact with a large
reservoir; the reservoir has conserved total energy $U_\text{tot}$ and total
particle number $N_\text{tot}$.  If the system is in microstate $s_j$ with
energy $E_j$ and particle number $N_j$, then the reservoir has energy
$U_\text{tot} - E_j$ and particle number $N_\text{tot} - N_j$.  The probability
of the system being in microstate $j$ is proportional to the number of reservoir
microstates, which is $e^{S_R(U_\text{tot}-E_j,\, N_\text{tot}-N_j)/k_B}$.

Since the reservoir is large, we Taylor expand its entropy in both arguments
about $(U_\text{tot}, N_\text{tot})$:
\begin{align}
    S_R(U_\text{tot} - E_j,\, N_\text{tot} - N_j)
    &\approx S_R(U_\text{tot}, N_\text{tot})
      - E_j\left.\pdv{S_R}{U}\right|_N
      - N_j\left.\pdv{S_R}{N}\right|_U.
\end{align}
Using the thermodynamic identities $(\partial S_R/\partial U)_N = 1/T$ and
$(\partial S_R/\partial N)_U = -\mu/T$, we obtain
\begin{equation}
    S_R \approx S_R(U_\text{tot}, N_\text{tot}) - \frac{E_j}{T} + \frac{\mu N_j}{T}.
\end{equation}
Exponentiating and absorbing the (constant) factor $e^{S_R(U_\text{tot},N_\text{tot})/k_B}$
into the normalisation gives the grand canonical probability:

\begin{formula}
\begin{equation}
    P_j(E_j, N_j) = \frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}}\,e^{-\beta(E_j - \mu N_j)},
\end{equation}
\end{formula}
where $\beta = 1/k_BT$ and the \textbf{grand partition function} is defined
by the normalisation condition.

\begin{definition}[Grand Partition Function]
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{Z} = \sum_j e^{-\beta(E_j - \mu N_j)} = \sum_j e^{-\beta E_j}\,e^{\beta\mu N_j}.
\end{equation}
The sum runs over all microstates $j$, each with its own energy $E_j$ and
particle number $N_j$.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Grand Potential and Thermodynamic Derivatives}

The connection to thermodynamics is made through the \textbf{grand potential}.

\begin{definition}[Grand Potential]
\begin{equation}
    \Omega \equiv -k_BT\ln\mathcal{Z} = U - TS - \mu N.
\end{equation}
The grand potential is the analogue of the Helmholtz free energy when particle
number is not conserved.  Its total differential follows from $dU = TdS - PdV + \mu\,dN$:
\begin{equation}
    d\Omega = -S\,dT - P\,dV - N\,d\mu.
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

From $d\Omega$ we read off the natural thermodynamic derivatives:

\begin{formula}
\begin{equation}
    S = -\left.\pdv{\Omega}{T}\right|_{V,\mu}, \qquad
    P = -\left.\pdv{\Omega}{V}\right|_{T,\mu}, \qquad
    \langle N\rangle = -\left.\pdv{\Omega}{\mu}\right|_{T,V}.
\end{equation}
\end{formula}

These can be verified directly from $\ln\mathcal{Z}$.  Differentiating with
respect to $\beta$ at fixed $\beta\mu$:
\begin{equation}
    \pdv{\ln\mathcal{Z}}{\beta}\bigg|_{\beta\mu} = \mu\langle N\rangle - \langle U\rangle.
\end{equation}
Differentiating with respect to $\beta\mu$ at fixed $\beta$:
\begin{equation}
    \pdv{\ln\mathcal{Z}}{(\beta\mu)}\bigg|_{\beta} = \langle N\rangle.
\end{equation}
The entropy is obtained from $S = -k_B\sum_j P_j \ln P_j$.  Substituting
$\ln P_j = \beta\mu N_j - \beta E_j - \ln\mathcal{Z}$ and simplifying yields
\begin{equation}
    S = \frac{\langle U\rangle - \mu\langle N\rangle}{T} + k_B\ln\mathcal{Z},
\end{equation}
which is entirely consistent with $\Omega = -k_BT\ln\mathcal{Z} = U - TS - \mu N$.

\subsection{The Modified First Law and Chemical Potential}

The modification of the first law to include particle exchange is
\begin{equation}
    dU = T\,dS - P\,dV + \mu\,dN.
\end{equation}

\begin{definition}[Chemical Potential]
The \textbf{chemical potential} $\mu$ is the energy cost of adding one particle
to the system at fixed entropy and volume:
\begin{equation}
    \mu = \pdv{U}{N}\bigg|_{S,V} = -T\pdv{S}{N}\bigg|_{U,V}.
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

The physical intuition is clear: consider two subsystems that can exchange both
energy and particles.  Maximising the total entropy with respect to energy
exchange gives the condition $T_1 = T_2$ (thermal equilibrium), and maximising
with respect to particle exchange gives
\begin{equation}
    \pdv{S_1}{N_1}\bigg|_{U,V} = \pdv{S_2}{N_2}\bigg|_{U,V}
    \implies \frac{\mu_1}{T_1} = \frac{\mu_2}{T_2}.
\end{equation}
At equal temperatures, equilibrium with respect to particle exchange therefore
requires $\mu_1 = \mu_2$.  The chemical potential plays exactly the same role
for particle exchange that temperature plays for energy exchange: particles flow
from high $\mu$ to low $\mu$, just as heat flows from high $T$ to low $T$.

\section{Fermi--Dirac and Bose--Einstein Statistics}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Factorisation of the Grand Partition Function}

For a system of non-interacting quantum particles, the grand partition function
acquires an elegant factorised form.  Label the single-particle energy levels
$\varepsilon_\alpha$ for $\alpha = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$.  A microstate of the
many-body system is entirely specified by the occupation numbers $\{n_\alpha\}$,
and the grand Boltzmann weight is
\begin{equation}
    e^{-\beta(E_j - \mu N_j)}
    = \exp\!\left(\sum_\alpha \beta(\mu - \varepsilon_\alpha)\,n_\alpha\right)
    = \prod_\alpha e^{\beta(\mu-\varepsilon_\alpha)n_\alpha}.
\end{equation}
Summing over all allowed configurations $\{n_\alpha\}$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{Z} = \sum_{\{n_\alpha\}} \prod_\alpha e^{\beta(\mu-\varepsilon_\alpha)n_\alpha}
    = \prod_\alpha \sum_{n_\alpha} e^{\beta(\mu-\varepsilon_\alpha)n_\alpha}
    \equiv \prod_\alpha \mathcal{Z}_\alpha.
\end{equation}
This factorisation is the key advantage of the grand canonical ensemble: because
no fixed-$N$ constraint couples the levels, the sums over individual occupation
numbers decouple completely.

\subsection{Fermions: the Fermi--Dirac Distribution}

For fermions, the Pauli exclusion principle restricts $n_\alpha \in \{0, 1\}$.
The single-level sum is therefore just two terms:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{Z}_\alpha^{\text{(F)}} = 1 + e^{\beta(\mu-\varepsilon_\alpha)}.
\end{equation}
The full fermionic grand partition function is
\begin{formula}
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{Z} = \prod_\alpha \Bigl(1 + e^{\beta(\mu-\varepsilon_\alpha)}\Bigr).
\end{equation}
\end{formula}
To find the mean occupation of level $\alpha$, differentiate
$\ln\mathcal{Z} = \sum_\alpha \ln(1 + e^{\beta(\mu-\varepsilon_\alpha)})$ with
respect to $\mu$ at fixed $\beta$:
\begin{equation}
    \langle n_\alpha\rangle
    = \frac{e^{\beta(\mu-\varepsilon_\alpha)}}{1 + e^{\beta(\mu-\varepsilon_\alpha)}}.
\end{equation}

\begin{formula}
\textbf{Fermi--Dirac distribution:}
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\langle n_\alpha \rangle_{\text{FD}}
    = \frac{1}{e^{\beta(\varepsilon_\alpha - \mu)} + 1}.}
\end{equation}
\end{formula}

\subsection{Bosons: the Bose--Einstein Distribution}

For bosons there is no restriction on occupation numbers; each $n_\alpha$ ranges
over all non-negative integers.  The single-level sum is a geometric series:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{Z}_\alpha^{\text{(B)}}
    = \sum_{n_\alpha=0}^\infty e^{\beta(\mu-\varepsilon_\alpha)n_\alpha}
    = \frac{1}{1 - e^{\beta(\mu-\varepsilon_\alpha)}},
\end{equation}
which converges provided $\mu < \varepsilon_\alpha$ for all $\alpha$, i.e.\ the
chemical potential must lie strictly below the ground-state energy.  The bosonic
grand partition function is
\begin{formula}
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{Z} = \prod_\alpha \frac{1}{1 - e^{\beta(\mu-\varepsilon_\alpha)}}.
\end{equation}
\end{formula}
Differentiating $\ln\mathcal{Z} = -\sum_\alpha \ln(1 - e^{\beta(\mu-\varepsilon_\alpha)})$
with respect to $\mu$ gives the mean occupation:

\begin{formula}
\textbf{Bose--Einstein distribution:}
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\langle n_\alpha \rangle_{\text{BE}}
    = \frac{1}{e^{\beta(\varepsilon_\alpha - \mu)} - 1}.}
\end{equation}
\end{formula}

\subsection{Classical Limit and the Maxwell--Boltzmann Distribution}

At high energies or high temperatures, $\varepsilon_\alpha \gg \mu$ and therefore
$e^{\beta(\varepsilon_\alpha - \mu)} \gg 1$.  In this limit the $\pm 1$ in the
denominator is negligible and both distributions reduce to
\begin{equation}
    \langle n_\alpha\rangle \approx e^{-\beta(\varepsilon_\alpha - \mu)},
\end{equation}
the classical Maxwell--Boltzmann result.  The quantum corrections are negligible
when $\beta(\varepsilon - \mu) \gg 1$.

Quantum statistics become relevant precisely when $\langle n_\alpha\rangle \sim 1$
for the lowest-lying levels.  In the canonical approximation, the mean occupation
of a state in a box of volume $V$ is roughly $N/Z_1 = N\lambda_\text{th}^3 / V$,
where
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_\text{th} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2\pi m k_BT}}
\end{equation}
is the thermal de Broglie wavelength.  When this ratio approaches unity, the
quantum regime is entered.

\begin{definition}[Quantum Degeneracy Criterion]
A gas of $N$ particles in volume $V$ is \emph{quantum degenerate} when
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N}{V}\,\lambda_\text{th}^3 \sim 1.
\end{equation}
This condition is reached either by lowering the temperature (so that
$\lambda_\text{th}$ grows) or by increasing the density.  In the opposite
limit $N\lambda_\text{th}^3 \ll V$, the classical approximation $Z_N = Z_1^N/N!$
is valid and quantum statistics can be ignored.
\end{definition}

\section{The Photon Gas}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Physical Setup and Allowed Modes}

Consider an empty cubic box of side $L$ held at temperature $T$.  The walls
continuously absorb and emit photons, maintaining thermal equilibrium.  Photons
are bosons with spin 1; crucially, the number of photons in the box is not
conserved --- the walls act as a reservoir at fixed $T$ that can create or
destroy photons at will.  This means the chemical potential of photons is
$\mu = 0$, since there is no energy cost associated with changing the number.

To find the allowed modes, we solve Maxwell's equations in vacuum.  In the
absence of charges or currents,
\begin{align}
    \left(\frac{1}{c^2}\partial_t^2 - \nabla^2\right)\vec{E} = 0, \qquad
    \nabla\cdot\vec{E} = 0.
\end{align}
Seeking standing-wave solutions consistent with the conducting boundary condition
$E_\parallel = 0$ at each wall gives
\begin{align}
    E_x &= E_{x0}\cos(k_x x)\sin(k_y y)\sin(k_z z)\sin(\omega t),\notag\\
    E_y &= E_{y0}\sin(k_x x)\cos(k_y y)\sin(k_z z)\sin(\omega t),\\
    E_z &= E_{z0}\sin(k_x x)\sin(k_y y)\cos(k_z z)\sin(\omega t).\notag
\end{align}
The dispersion relation is $\omega = c|\vec{k}|$.  The transversality condition
$\nabla\cdot\vec{E} = 0$ imposes $k_x E_{x0} + k_y E_{y0} + k_z E_{z0} = 0$,
which removes one component of $\vec{E}_0$, leaving \emph{two} independent
polarisation states per wavevector.

\begin{formula}
The allowed wavevectors are
\begin{equation}
    \vec{k} = \frac{\pi}{L}(n_x,\,n_y,\,n_z), \qquad n_x, n_y, n_z = 0, 1, 2, \ldots,
\end{equation}
with two independent polarisation modes for each $\vec{k}$.
\end{formula}

Each electromagnetic mode of wavevector $\vec{k}$ satisfies
$\partial_t^2\vec{E} = -\omega^2\vec{E}$, which is the harmonic oscillator
equation.  Quantising each mode, the energy levels are
$\mathcal{E}_n(\vec{k}) = (n + \tfrac{1}{2})\hbar\omega(\vec{k})$, where $n$
counts the number of photons in that mode.  The full state of the electromagnetic
field is specified by the set of occupation numbers $\{n_{\vec{k},\lambda}\}$
for every mode $(\vec{k},\lambda)$.

\begin{definition}
The \textbf{total energy of the electromagnetic field} in a given state is
\begin{equation}
    E(\text{state}) = \sum_{\vec{k},\lambda}
    \left(n_{\vec{k},\lambda}+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\hbar\omega(\vec{k}).
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\subsection{Partition Function and Internal Energy}

Because each mode is an independent quantum harmonic oscillator, the partition
function factorises over modes.  Summing over all configurations
$\{n_{\vec{k},\lambda}\}$:
\begin{equation}
    Z = \prod_{\vec{k},\lambda}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
    e^{-\beta(n+\frac{1}{2})\hbar\omega(\vec{k})}
    = \prod_{\vec{k},\lambda} Z_\omega(\vec{k}), \qquad
    Z_\omega = \frac{e^{-\beta\hbar\omega/2}}{1-e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}}.
\end{equation}
In the thermodynamic limit, the discrete sum over mode indices becomes an
integral over the positive octant of $\vec{n}$-space.  Switching to spherical
coordinates and changing variable to $\omega = \pi c n/L$, and including the
factor of two for polarisations, gives
\begin{equation}
    \ln Z = \frac{V}{\pi^2 c^3}\int_0^\infty \omega^2
    \left[-\frac{\beta\hbar\omega}{2}
          -\ln\!\left(1-e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}\right)\right]d\omega.
\end{equation}
The zero-point term ($-\beta\hbar\omega/2$) contributes a temperature-independent
(though divergent) constant to the free energy; since it has no observable
thermodynamic consequence it is discarded.

\begin{formula}
\textbf{Log-partition function of the photon gas (dropping zero-point energy):}
\begin{equation}
    \ln Z = -\frac{V}{\pi^2 c^3}\int_0^\infty \omega^2
    \ln\!\left(1-e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}\right)\,d\omega.
\end{equation}
\end{formula}

The internal energy follows from $U = -\partial_\beta \ln Z$:
\begin{equation}
    U = \frac{V}{\pi^2 c^3}\int_0^\infty
    \underbrace{\omega^2}_{\text{density of modes}}\;
    \underbrace{\frac{\hbar\omega}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega}-1}}_{\text{mean energy per mode}}
    \,d\omega.
\end{equation}
The integrand is the \textbf{Planck distribution}: each mode of frequency
$\omega$ contributes mean energy $\hbar\omega/(e^{\beta\hbar\omega}-1)$, which
is precisely $\hbar\omega$ times the Bose--Einstein occupation number with
$\mu = 0$.

To evaluate the total energy, substitute $\tau = \beta\hbar\omega$:
\begin{equation}
    U = \frac{\hbar V}{\pi^2 c^3}\!\left(\frac{k_BT}{\hbar}\right)^{\!4}
    \int_0^\infty \frac{\tau^3\,d\tau}{e^\tau-1}.
\end{equation}
The integral is a standard result expressible in terms of the Riemann zeta
function.

\begin{fact}
\textbf{Riemann zeta function:} The identity
$\int_0^\infty x^s (e^x - 1)^{-1}\,dx = s!\;\zeta(s+1)$ gives
\begin{equation}
    \int_0^\infty \frac{\tau^3\,d\tau}{e^\tau-1} = 3!\;\zeta(4)
    = 6\cdot\frac{\pi^4}{90} = \frac{\pi^4}{15}.
\end{equation}
The relevant zeta values are $\zeta(2) = \pi^2/6$, $\zeta(3)\approx 1.202$,
$\zeta(4) = \pi^4/90$.
\end{fact}

Inserting this result:

\begin{formula}
\textbf{Energy density of the electromagnetic field (Stefan's $T^4$ law):}
\begin{equation}
    \frac{U}{V} = \frac{\pi^2 k_B^4}{15\hbar^3 c^3}\,T^4 = \frac{4\sigma}{c}\,T^4,
    \qquad \sigma \equiv \frac{\pi^2 k_B^4}{60\hbar^3 c^2}.
\end{equation}
\end{formula}

The free energy follows by integrating by parts in the frequency integral,
giving $F = -U/3$.  From $dF = -S\,dT - P\,dV$ the pressure and entropy are
\begin{equation}
    P = -\pdv{F}{V}\bigg|_T = \frac{U}{3V} = \frac{4\sigma T^4}{3c},
\end{equation}
\begin{formula}
\textbf{Entropy of the photon gas:}
\begin{equation}
    S = -\pdv{F}{T}\bigg|_V = \frac{4U}{3T} = \frac{16\sigma T^3 V}{3c}.
\end{equation}
\end{formula}
The heat capacity is $C_V = \partial U/\partial T|_V = 16\sigma T^3 V/c$, growing
as $T^3$.  Radiation pressure equals one-third the energy density, precisely as
for a classical gas of massless particles.  The number of photons scales as
$N \propto T^3$: unlike conserved-particle systems, the photon number adjusts
freely with temperature.

\subsection{Stefan--Boltzmann Law and Spectral Radiance}

Imagine a small hole in the box.  Photons incident on the hole from within
a hemisphere escape; integrating over all directions and all frequencies
yields the power emitted per unit area.  The spectral radiance per unit angular
frequency is
\begin{fact}
\begin{equation}
    \frac{P/A}{\text{unit }\omega}
    = \frac{\hbar}{4\pi^2 c^2}\cdot\frac{\omega^3}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega}-1}.
\end{equation}
\end{fact}
Integrating over $\omega$ and using $\int_0^\infty \tau^3/(e^\tau-1)\,d\tau = \pi^4/15$:

\begin{theorem}[Stefan--Boltzmann Law]
The total power per unit area emitted by a blackbody is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{P}{A} = \sigma T^4, \qquad
    \sigma = \frac{\pi^2 k_B^4}{60\hbar^3 c^2} \approx 5.67\times 10^{-8}
    \,\text{W\,m}^{-2}\text{K}^{-4}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

One can also verify $P = U/(3V)$ mechanically: for a plane wave incident on a
wall at angle $\theta$ to the wall normal, each photon imparts impulse
$2\hbar|\vec{k}|\cos\theta$ to the wall upon reflection, and only the normal
component contributes to pressure.  Averaging over the hemisphere gives
$\langle\cos^2\theta\rangle = 1/3$, consistent with the thermodynamic result.

\subsection{Classical Limit and the Ultraviolet Catastrophe}

As $\hbar\to 0$, the Bose--Einstein denominator becomes
$e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1 \approx \beta\hbar\omega$, so the mean energy per
mode approaches $k_BT$ (the classical equipartition value).  The energy density
then diverges:
\begin{equation}
    U \to \frac{k_BTV}{\pi^2 c^3}\int_0^\infty \omega^2\,d\omega \to \infty.
\end{equation}
This is the \textbf{ultraviolet catastrophe}: the classical Rayleigh--Jeans
law assigns $k_BT$ to every mode, and since there are infinitely many
high-frequency modes, the total energy diverges.  Planck's resolution --- that
each mode can only absorb or emit energy in quanta $\hbar\omega$ --- was a
foundational step in the development of quantum mechanics.

\subsection{Application: Equilibrium Temperature of the Earth}

The Sun has surface temperature $T_0 \approx 5772\,\text{K}$ and radius
$R_\odot \approx 6.96\times 10^8\,\text{m}$; the Earth--Sun distance is
$a \approx 1.5\times 10^{11}\,\text{m}$.  The total solar luminosity is
$P_\odot = 4\pi R_\odot^2\,\sigma T_0^4$, and the power intercepted by the
Earth's cross-section $\pi R_E^2$ is
\begin{equation}
    P_\text{in} = P_\odot \cdot \frac{\pi R_E^2}{4\pi a^2}.
\end{equation}
Setting this equal to the power radiated by the Earth's entire surface,
$P_\text{out} = 4\pi R_E^2\,\sigma T_E^4$, and cancelling $R_E$ gives:

\begin{formula}
\textbf{Earth's radiative equilibrium temperature:}
\begin{equation}
    T_E = T_0\!\left(\frac{R_\odot}{2a}\right)^{\!1/2} \approx 278\,\text{K}.
\end{equation}
This is close to the observed mean surface temperature; the actual value is
higher due to the greenhouse effect, which traps some of the emitted infrared
radiation.
\end{formula}

\section{Degenerate Fermi Systems}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{The Zero-Temperature Fermi Gas}

At zero temperature the Fermi--Dirac distribution becomes a sharp step function:
\begin{equation}
    \langle n_\alpha\rangle \xrightarrow{T\to 0}
    \begin{cases}
        1 & \varepsilon_\alpha < \mu, \\
        0 & \varepsilon_\alpha > \mu.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
All single-particle states below the chemical potential are fully occupied and
all states above are empty.  The chemical potential at $T = 0$ is called the
\textbf{Fermi energy} $\varepsilon_F$, and the surface in energy space at
$\varepsilon = \mu$ is the \textbf{Fermi surface}.

\varepsilon_F$ and 0 for $\varepsilon > \varepsilon_F$; thermally broadened

$\sim k_BT$ near $\varepsilon_F$]
\end{center}

To find $\varepsilon_F$, we use the condition that all $N$ particles must fit
into the states below $\varepsilon_F$.  For spin-$\tfrac{1}{2}$ particles
(two spin states) in a box of volume $V$, the density of states in three
dimensions is $g(\varepsilon) \propto \sqrt{\varepsilon}$, and the normalisation
condition gives
\begin{equation}
    N = \int_0^{\varepsilon_F} g(\varepsilon)\,d\varepsilon \propto \varepsilon_F^{3/2}.
\end{equation}
This determines $\varepsilon_F \propto (N/V)^{2/3}$: the Fermi energy is higher
for denser systems.  The physical picture at $T = 0$ is of a \emph{Fermi sea} of
filled states up to the sharp Fermi surface.  Adding one more particle requires
placing it just above the surface, costing energy $\varepsilon_F$: this is the
chemical potential at zero temperature, $\mu(T=0) = \varepsilon_F$.  The total
internal energy at $T = 0$ is large and extensive, since particles fill all states
up to $\varepsilon_F$.

\subsection{Low-Temperature Corrections: the Sommerfeld Expansion}

At temperatures small compared to the Fermi temperature $T_F \equiv \varepsilon_F/k_B$,
the Fermi--Dirac step is only slightly smeared.  A systematic expansion in
powers of $k_BT/\varepsilon_F$ (the Sommerfeld expansion) yields low-temperature
corrections to the thermodynamic quantities.  The internal energy acquires a
leading correction
\begin{equation}
    U(T) \approx U(0) + \frac{\pi^2}{6}g(\varepsilon_F)(k_BT)^2 + O(T^4),
\end{equation}
where $g(\varepsilon_F)$ is the density of states at the Fermi level.  This
gives a heat capacity linear in temperature,
\begin{equation}
    C_V \approx \frac{\pi^2}{3}k_B^2 T\, g(\varepsilon_F) \propto T,
\end{equation}
in stark contrast to the classical value $C_V = \tfrac{3}{2}Nk_B$ which is
independent of temperature.  The linear-$T$ specific heat is a hallmark of a
degenerate Fermi system and is observed experimentally in metals at low temperatures.

\begin{fact}
\textbf{Conduction electrons in metals.}  The conduction electrons in a metal
are well approximated as a degenerate Fermi gas.  For copper, the number density
$n \approx 8.5\times10^{28}\,\text{m}^{-3}$ and $\varepsilon_F \approx 7\,\text{eV}$,
corresponding to a Fermi temperature $T_F \approx 81\,000\,\text{K}$.  At room
temperature $T \approx 300\,\text{K}$, we have $T/T_F \approx 0.004$, so the
electron gas is deeply degenerate.  This explains why electrons contribute only
a small, linearly temperature-dependent fraction to the heat capacity of metals:
most electrons cannot be thermally excited because all nearby states are already
occupied.  The same physics underlies the electrical and thermal conductivity
of metals, and, on astrophysical scales, the stability of white dwarf stars
(supported by electron degeneracy pressure) and neutron stars (supported by
neutron degeneracy pressure).
\end{fact}

\section{Bose--Einstein Condensation}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{The Maximum Density of Excited States}

The Bose--Einstein distribution requires $\mu < \varepsilon_0$ (where
$\varepsilon_0$ is the ground-state energy, taken to be zero) for all occupation
numbers to be positive and finite.  As $T$ decreases at fixed $N/V$, the chemical
potential $\mu$ rises toward $0$.  We ask: how many particles can be accommodated
in the excited states when $\mu = 0$, the maximum possible value?

For a non-interacting Bose gas in a box of volume $V$, replacing the sum over
states by an integral over the three-dimensional density of states
$g(\varepsilon) \propto \sqrt{\varepsilon}$ and setting $\mu = 0$:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_\text{excited, max}}{V}
    = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\cdot\frac{1}{\lambda_\text{th}^3}
      \int_0^\infty \frac{\sqrt{u}\,du}{e^u - 1}
    = \frac{\zeta(3/2)}{\lambda_\text{th}^3},
\end{equation}
where $\zeta(3/2) \approx 2.612$.  This integral converges because the density
of states $g(\varepsilon) \propto \sqrt{\varepsilon}$ vanishes faster than the
Bose--Einstein divergence at $\varepsilon \to 0$.  The result is remarkable: at
any given temperature, the excited states can hold at most a \emph{finite} number
density of particles.

A subtle but important point is that the density of states $g(\varepsilon_0) = 0$
at the ground-state energy, so the ground state is entirely absent from the
continuum integral: a macroscopic occupation of the ground state cannot be captured
by replacing sums with integrals.  The ground-state occupation must be treated as
a separate term.

\subsection{Condensation and the Condensate Fraction}

The total particle number is the sum of the ground-state occupation $n_0$ and
the number in excited states:
\begin{equation}
    N = n_0 + V\lambda_\text{th}^{-3}\,\zeta(3/2).
\end{equation}
When $N/V < \zeta(3/2)/\lambda_\text{th}^3$ (high temperature or low density),
$\mu < 0$ adjusts to accommodate all particles in excited states and $n_0 \approx 0$:
the system is not condensed.  But as $T$ is lowered at fixed $N/V$, $\lambda_\text{th}$
grows, and eventually the excited states can no longer hold all $N$ particles.
At that point $\mu$ saturates at $0$ and the remaining particles are forced into
the ground state: this is \textbf{Bose--Einstein condensation}.

\begin{fact}[Bose--Einstein Condensation]
When $N/V > \zeta(3/2)/\lambda_\text{th}^3$, a macroscopic fraction of the bosons
condenses into the single-particle ground state.  The condensate fraction is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{n_0}{N} = 1 - \frac{V\lambda_\text{th}^{-3}\zeta(3/2)}{N}
    = 1 - \left(\frac{T}{T_c}\right)^{3/2},
\end{equation}
where the \textbf{condensation temperature} $T_c$ is defined by
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N}{V} = \frac{\zeta(3/2)}{\lambda_\text{th}^3(T_c)},
    \qquad \text{i.e.} \quad
    k_BT_c = \frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{m}\!\left(\frac{N}{V\,\zeta(3/2)}\right)^{\!2/3}.
\end{equation}
Below $T_c$, the condensate fraction grows continuously from zero, signalling a
genuine phase transition driven entirely by quantum statistics rather than
interactions.
\end{fact}

\end{center}

Unlike classical phase transitions, BEC occurs even in an ideal, non-interacting
gas: it is a purely quantum-statistical effect, driven by the tendency of bosons
to cluster in low-energy states.  The onset of condensation is associated with
the development of long-range phase coherence across the macroscopic ground-state
occupation.

\begin{fact}[Experimental realisation]
Bose--Einstein condensation was first observed experimentally in 1995 by Cornell,
Wieman, and Ketterle in dilute gases of alkali atoms (rubidium and sodium) cooled
to temperatures of order $100\,\text{nK}$ by laser cooling and evaporative
cooling.  At these temperatures the thermal de Broglie wavelength becomes
comparable to the inter-particle spacing, and a macroscopic fraction of the atoms
piles up in the motional ground state of the trap.  The resulting condensate
exhibits striking quantum coherence: two condensates interfere like laser beams,
and vortices quantised in units of $\hbar$ can be nucleated in a rotating
condensate.  Later experiments explored the crossover to fermionic superfluidity,
connecting BEC to Bardeen--Cooper--Schrieffer (BCS) pairing, and establishing
a unified picture of quantum degeneracy phenomena across both statistics.
\end{fact}
